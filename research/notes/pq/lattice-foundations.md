# Lattice foundations: from lattices to LWE, Module-LWE, LWR and NTRU

This file explains the lattice mathematics that every lattice KEM rests on, from a basis and its determinant up to LWE, Ring-LWE, Module-LWE, LWR and NTRU, and it records what the hardness reductions do and do not promise for concrete parameters. It also pins down what "dimension about 1000" means for each scheme family, with a table of real instances taken from primary specifications. Date checked: 2026-09-27. All scripts named here were run in this session and end with "all checks passed" (toy_lwe 14 checks, toy_mlwe 11, lattice_basics 17, lf_error_distributions 23, lf_structured_toys 9, lf_ntt_and_dimensions 21); commands are in the claim ledger. The checks are not vacuous: during this run three of them failed first and were corrected against the sources (FrodoKEM-1344's table std had been compared with sigma instead of the rounded Gaussian's std; NewHope's printed 1.00063 is a truncation of 1.0006357; the pure-GSA primal model differs from Kyber's official BKZ simulation by about 4 block sizes), and the scripts contain negative controls (noisy elimination, a bad basis, an undersized NTRU modulus). Earlier-run scripts lattice_basics.py, toy_lwe.py and toy_mlwe.py were re-read and re-run; toy_lwe.py was corrected to use Regev's own decoder and an exact mod-q failure sum. No claim in this file is UNVERIFIED; unresolved points are listed under Open questions.

## Key findings

1. **"About 1000 dimensions" is four different numbers.** Plain-LWE secret dimension n (FrodoKEM-976: 976), Module-LWE rank times ring degree (ML-KEM-1024: 4 x 256 = 1024), ring degree for Ring-LWE/NTRU (NewHope1024: 1024; sntrup1013: 1013), and the dimension of the lattice the attacker reduces, about m + n + 1 (ML-KEM-1024: 1885; FrodoKEM-976: 1969 in the refined estimate). The cost is set by the BKZ block size (878 for ML-KEM-1024 under core-SVP), not by n. [L90]-[L93], [C34]-[C35]
2. **A ~1000-dimension NIST lattice KEM already exists: ML-KEM-1024** (FIPS 203: n = 256, q = 3329, k = 4, CBD(2), 1568-byte key and ciphertext). The best-studied unstructured alternative near 1000 dimensions is FrodoKEM-976 (plain LWE, q = 2^16, sigma = 2.3, 15.6 KB public key), which NIST dropped because of its performance, not a security finding (IR 8413-upd1 Sec. 2.3). [L50], [L95]-[L96], [L100]
3. **Worst-case to average-case reductions do not give concrete security.** They are non-tight and require Gaussian errors of std >= about sqrt(n)/(2 pi) (about 5 at n = 1000), while every deployed scheme uses std 1.0-2.8. Kyber's and FrodoKEM's own specifications base their security levels on attack-cost estimates and say so. [L34]-[L40], [C25]
4. **What the reductions do say.** Plain LWE: quantum (Regev) and classical (Peikert for q >= 2^n; BLPRS for polynomial q with a dimension loss n -> sqrt(n)). Ring-LWE: quantum, from ideal lattices only. Module-LWE: quantum, from module lattices, with converse reductions. The structured problems rest on narrower worst-case problems than plain LWE. [L27]-[L31], [L33]
5. **Structure has not helped attacks on Module-LWE at deployed parameters**, per Kyber's and FrodoKEM's specifications, but it has broken neighbouring problems: short generators of principal ideals (CDPR16), approximate ideal-SVP at 2^(O~(sqrt n)) factors, overstretched NTRU (ABD16, KF17), and Ring-LWE instances with too-narrow errors (Peikert 2016). None of these reaches ML-KEM or the NTRU KEMs, for stated reasons. [L62]-[L73]
6. **NTRU is structurally weaker than Ring-LWE in one known way**: its lattice has N short vectors (all rotations of the key), a dense sublattice that attacks exploit once q is large; the concrete fatigue point is q ~ 0.004 n^2.484, about 6 to 21 times above the q the NTRU round-3 KEMs use. Kirchner-Fouque recommend Ring-LWE over NTRU where the cost is small. [L70]-[L72], [C14]
7. **Error shape matters little, width matters a lot.** Known attacks depend on the error's standard deviation (and possibly entropy), not its exact shape; binomial and table-approximated Gaussians are covered by Renyi-divergence arguments (NewHope's R_9 ~ 1.00063 and FrodoKEM's D_alpha ~ 1e-5, both recomputed). Width trades security against decryption failure through q. Very narrow errors with many samples are breakable (Arora-Ge). [L81]-[L86], [C20]-[C22]
8. **LWR (rounding instead of noise) has weaker proofs**: equivalence to LWE needs a super-polynomial modulus ratio (BPR12) or a bounded number of samples (AKPW13, BGMRR16). Saber (Module-LWR) was a finalist and was not selected. [L51]-[L55], [L100]
9. **q = 3329 and n = 256 are an engineering choice**: n = 256 carries a 256-bit key; q is the smallest prime = 1 mod 256 that gives negligible decryption failure (257 and 769 are too small); X^256 + 1 then splits into 128 irreducible quadratics (an incomplete NTT). A complete NTT at n = 1024 needs q = 1 mod 2048 (12289, as in NewHope). Power-of-two moduli and NTRU Prime's inert x^p - x - 1 have no NTT. The NTT is a representation; it does not change hardness. [L87]-[L89], [C30]-[C33]
10. **Normal form is free**: a secret drawn from the error distribution is as hard as a uniform one (ACPS09 Lemma 2), which is why all schemes use small secrets. [L25], [C40]
11. **A claimed quantum polynomial-time LWE algorithm (Chen, April 2024) was withdrawn by its author the same month** after a bug was found in step 9. Status of quantum attacks: quantum-crypto-and-quantum-attacks.md. [L101]

## 1. Lattices from first principles

A **lattice** is the set of all integer combinations of n linearly independent vectors b1..bn:
L(B) = { x1 b1 + ... + xn bn : xi integers }. The vectors form a **basis** B (MR09 p.4-5, [L2]).

- **Many bases, one lattice.** If U is an integer matrix with det U = +-1 (unimodular), B and BU generate the same lattice, and two bases generate the same lattice only in that case (MR09 p.5, [L3]). Worked example (lattice_basics.py Part A): the "good" basis rows (5,1), (-2,6) and the "bad" basis rows (29,25), (37,33) = U times good, with U = [[7,3],[9,4]], det U = 1. Both describe exactly the same set of points.
- **Determinant (volume).** det L = |det B|, independent of the basis; it is the volume of one cell and the inverse density of lattice points (MR09 p.5, [L4]). Both example bases have |det| = 32.
- **Gram-Schmidt.** b1* = b1, and bi* is bi minus its projections on b1*..b(i-1)*, with coefficients mu_ij = <bi, bj*>/<bj*, bj*> (LLL82 eq. 1.2-1.3, [L5]). The volume equals the product of the Gram-Schmidt lengths, d(L) = prod |bi*| (LLL82 proof of 1.6, p.517, [L6]). Example: good basis GS lengths 5.099 and 6.276; bad basis 38.288 and 0.836. Both products are 32. A good basis has GS lengths of similar size; a bad basis has one very long and one very short. Lattice reduction is the art of evening out this profile.
- **Successive minima.** lambda_1 is the length of a shortest non-zero lattice vector; lambda_i is the smallest r such that the ball of radius r contains i linearly independent lattice vectors (standard; used in Regev 2009 and LS15). Example: lambda_1 = 5.0990 at (-5,-1), lambda_2 = 6.3246 at (-2,6) (COMPUTED, [C1]).
- **Minkowski's bound.** Every n-dimensional lattice has a non-zero vector of length at most sqrt(n) vol(L)^(1/n) (Kirchner-Fouque 2017, Theorem 1, PDF p.7, [L7]). Example: 5.099 <= sqrt(2) sqrt(32) = 8. It is an upper bound for every lattice; the Gaussian heuristic below is a prediction for random ones.
- **Gaussian heuristic.** For a "random" lattice, lambda_1 is about the radius of a ball whose volume equals det L, which for large n is about sqrt(n / (2 pi e)) det(L)^(1/n) (MR09 p.6, [L8]). It is a heuristic, not a theorem, and is poor in dimension 2 (example: exact-ball 3.19 vs true 5.10), but good for random q-ary lattices already at m = 12: over 12 random lattices Lambda_q(A) with m = 12, n = 6, q = 97 the ratio lambda_1/GH averaged 1.017 (COMPUTED, [C2]).
- **q-ary lattices.** For A in Z_q^(n x m): Lambda_q(A) = { y in Z^m : y = A^T s mod q } and Lambda_q^perp(A) = { y : A y = 0 mod q }. They are dual up to scaling by q. For prime q and full-rank A, det Lambda_q^perp(A) = q^n (MR09 p.5-6, [L9]); hence det Lambda_q(A) = q^(m-n) (COMPUTED check in lattice_basics.py Part C). Almost every lattice in lattice cryptography is a q-ary lattice.

## 2. Hard lattice problems

Definitions from MR09 Section 2 (p.5, [L10]) and Regev 2009:

- **SVP**: given a basis, find a shortest non-zero vector. **SVP_gamma**: find a non-zero vector at most gamma times lambda_1.
- **CVP**: given a basis and a target t, find the lattice point closest to t.
- **SIVP**: find n linearly independent lattice vectors minimising the longest one. **SIVP_gamma**: within gamma of the optimum lambda_n.
- **GapSVP_gamma**: the decision version: decide whether lambda_1 <= 1 or lambda_1 > gamma (Regev 2009 uses GapSVP and SIVP in Theorem 1.1).
- **BDD** (bounded distance decoding): CVP when the target is promised to be much closer to the lattice than lambda_1, so the answer is unique. LWE is a BDD instance on a q-ary lattice (section 4).
- **uSVP_gamma** (unique SVP): SVP when lambda_2 > gamma lambda_1, so the shortest vector is unique up to sign. The primal attack turns LWE into uSVP (section 11).

The basis is the trapdoor. Babai rounding with the good basis decodes a BDD target (20.2, -9.9) to the right lattice point (19, -9); the same method with the bad basis of the same lattice returns (3, -25) (COMPUTED, lattice_basics.py Part B, [C3]).

## 3. Lattice reduction in outline (LLL, BKZ)

LLL (1982) outputs a basis with |mu_ij| <= 1/2 (size reduction, eq. 1.4) and the Lovasz condition |bi* + mu_i,i-1 b(i-1)*|^2 >= (3/4)|b(i-1)*|^2 (eq. 1.5), in polynomial time. It guarantees |b1| <= 2^((n-1)/4) d(L)^(1/n) (eq. 1.9), and the constant 3/4 may be any y with 1/4 < y < 1 (LLL82 p.516-517, [L11]). In practice LLL does much better than the bound. BKZ with block size b calls an SVP solver in dimension b on projected blocks; larger b gives shorter vectors at a cost exponential in b. The Kyber round-3 specification models BKZ output by the geometric series assumption with root-Hermite factor delta = ((pi b)^(1/b) b / (2 pi e))^(1/(2(b-1))) (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 5.1.2, p.26, [L12]). Costs belong to attack-cost-estimation.md.

The quality measure is the **root-Hermite factor** delta: the reduced basis's first vector has length about delta^d vol(L)^(1/d) in dimension d. In practice LLL reaches delta_0 about 1.0219 [Gama-Nguyen 2008] (APS15 Sec. 3, PDF p.8, [L13]); MR09 (a 2008 text) quoted delta about 1.01-1.013 for the best practical algorithms of that time and called 1.005 "totally out of reach" in dimension 500 (MR09 p.6, [L14]); that judgement is dated, since today's estimates for ML-KEM-1024 assume BKZ block sizes near 878 ([L92]). Worked numbers (lattice_basics.py Part D, [C4]): on random 10- to 40-dimensional lattices our LLL gives delta between 0.993 and 1.015, far inside the worst-case bound 2^((n-1)/4). Part E runs the primal attack of section 11 with LLL alone on toy LWE (q = 97, m = 2n, CBD(1)): LLL recovers (s, e, 1) in all 3 trials up to d = 121 (n = 40) and in none at d = 145 (n = 48), although the gap between the Gaussian heuristic and the target stays near 7 ([C5]). What LLL can resolve shrinks as d grows; beyond that, BKZ with ever larger blocks is needed.

**Why the dual picture matters.** LWE samples (A, b = A s + e) put b very close to the q-ary lattice Lambda_q(A^T) generated by the columns of A, at distance ||e||, much smaller than lambda_1: decoding b is BDD, and the "primal" attack turns BDD into uSVP by adding b as an extra basis vector (Kannan embedding). The "dual" attack instead finds a short vector in the dual lattice and uses it to distinguish (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 5.1.3, p.26, [L103]).

## 4. LWE

**Definition (Regev 2009, Sec. 2, PDF p.15 of the full version, [L20]).** Fix a dimension n, a modulus q (Regev writes p) and an error distribution chi on Z_q. For a secret s in Z_q^n, the LWE distribution A_{s,chi} outputs pairs (a, <a,s> + e mod q) with a uniform in Z_q^n and e drawn from chi. With m samples stacked as rows, this is b = A s + e (mod q), A an m x n matrix.

- **Search-LWE**: given samples, find s.
- **Decision-LWE**: tell A_{s,chi} samples apart from uniform pairs in Z_q^n x Z_q.
- For prime q <= poly(n), decision and search are equivalent (Regev 2009 Lemma 4.2, "Decision to Search", which needs q prime; [L21]). Peikert 2009 repeats that no such equivalence was known for larger q (PDF p.2, [L22]). All the encryption schemes use the decision form.

**Why the noise matters.** Without e, b = A s is a linear system and Gaussian elimination solves it. With e, elimination mixes the small errors with large coefficients and returns garbage. toy_lwe.py Part A: n = 3, q = 97, m = 12, e from CBD(1). Elimination on b = A s returns the true s = [9, 5, 2]; on b = A s + e it returns [82, 39, 42]. Exhaustive search over all 97^3 = 912,673 secrets finds s (residual sum 6 versus 86 for the runner-up), but its cost q^n grows exponentially (COMPUTED, [C10]).

**Parameters.** n (secret dimension, the main security parameter), q (modulus), m (number of samples the attacker sees; bounded in real schemes: ML-KEM exposes m = (k+1)n samples per key, Kyber r3 Sec. 5.1.1 [L23]), and the width of chi (the standard deviation relative to q). Regev's own example setting was q = O(n^2) and alpha = 1/(sqrt(n) log^2 n), with the error a discretised Gaussian of standard deviation alpha q (Regev 2009 p.3, [L24]).

**Normal form (secret from the error distribution).** Applebaum, Cash, Peikert and Sahai (CRYPTO 2009, Lemma 2, Sec. 3.1, [L25]) give a deterministic polynomial-time transformation that, for prime-power q, maps A_{s,chi} with arbitrary s to A_{x,chi} with x drawn from chi^n, and maps uniform samples to uniform samples. The idea: take n samples with an invertible matrix A1 (so b1 = A1^T s + x), and rewrite every later sample (a, b) as a' = -A1^(-1) a, b' = b + <a', b1>. The new secret is x, which is small. Why it works: b' = <a,s> + e + <a', A1^T s + x> and A1 a' = -a, so the s terms cancel and b' = <a', x> + e with the original error e. Worked example (lf_structured_toys.py Part A, q = 97, n = 6, [C40]): a uniform secret s = [34, 80, 96, 84, 79, 20] becomes the new secret x = [0, -1, 1, 0, -1, 1], which is exactly the errors of the six kept samples, and 200 transformed samples all satisfy b' = <a', x> + e. The consequence: a secret drawn from the (small) error distribution is no easier than a uniform secret, so schemes may use small secrets. All of ML-KEM, FrodoKEM, Saber and NewHope use small secrets drawn like the errors. FrodoKEM's specification says its parameters are for the "normal-form (decisional) LWE problem" (FrodoKEM r3 spec Table 1-2 caption, p.24, [L26]).

## 5. Worst-case to average-case reductions: what they guarantee

A worst-case to average-case reduction says: an algorithm that breaks random instances of the average-case problem (LWE with random A) can be turned into an algorithm that solves a lattice problem on every lattice of some dimension. If some lattices are hard, random LWE instances are hard.

| result | direction and type | conditions (as stated) | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regev 2005/2009 | quantum reduction from worst-case GapSVP and SIVP within O~(n/alpha) to (search) LWE | alpha in (0,1), alpha q > 2 sqrt(n); error a discretised Gaussian Psi_alpha; q arbitrary integer (prime only for search-decision) | Regev 2009 Thm 1.1 (PDF p.2), Thm 3.1 (PDF p.18), [L27] |
| Peikert 2009 | classical reduction from worst-case GapSVP to search LWE | modulus q >= 2^n (exponential); for q >= omega(sqrt n) only a non-standard GapSVP variant | Peikert 2009 Sec. 1.1, PDF p.3 (version dated Nov 13, 2008), [L28] |
| Brakerski-Langlois-Peikert-Regev-Stehle 2013 | classical reduction for polynomial modulus | n-dimensional LWE with poly(n) modulus is as hard as a worst-case problem in dimension sqrt(n) (a quadratic loss); only GapSVP-type problems, not SIVP | BLPRS13 Thm 1.1 (informal), p.2-5, [L29] |
| Lyubashevsky-Peikert-Regev 2010/2013 | quantum reduction from worst-case approx-SVP on ideal lattices to search Ring-LWE, plus search-to-decision | cyclotomic rings; q = 1 mod m prime, poly(n) | LPR10 Sec. 1.1, p.3, [L30] |
| Langlois-Stehle 2015 | quantum reduction from worst-case problems on module lattices (Mod-GIVP / Mod-SIVP) to M-LWE, search and decision | alpha q > 2 sqrt(d) omega(sqrt(log n)), q of known factorisation; module lattice dimension N = n d | LS15 Thm 4.7 (p.16), Sec. 2 (p.8), [L31] |

What the reductions **do** give:
- Qualitative evidence that the LWE distribution has no built-in structural flaw. The FrodoKEM authors put it this way: reductions "help guide the search for cryptographically hard problems" and offer evidence "that the particular distribution of inputs does not introduce any fundamental structural weaknesses", in contrast to lattice proposals without reductions that were broken (FrodoKEM r3 spec Sec. 1.2.2, p.6, [L32]).
- A classical (BLPRS13) as well as quantum (Regev) basis for plain LWE; for Ring-LWE and Module-LWE only quantum reductions, and only from ideal or module lattices, not general lattices (LS15 abstract and p.3-4, [L33]).

What they **do not** give:
- **No concrete security number.** The reductions are non-tight: they lose polynomial factors in time and samples. FrodoKEM: the reduction "does not yield any meaningful 'end-to-end' security guarantee for our concrete parameters ... because the reduction is non-tight"; FrodoKEM uses it "only for guidance in choosing a narrow enough error distribution ... and not for any concrete security claim" (FrodoKEM r3 spec Sec. 1.2.2, p.7, [L34]).
- **The parameters used in practice are outside the theorems.** The quantum reductions need Gaussian error of standard deviation at least c sqrt(n), c > 1/(2 pi) (FrodoKEM r3 spec p.6, [L35]). For n = 976 that is about 5.0 or more (COMPUTED: sqrt(976)/(2 pi) = 4.97, [C11]), while FrodoKEM-976 uses sigma = 2.3; FrodoKEM therefore appeals to a different, classical reduction from "BDDwDGS" with a bounded number of samples (p.7, [L36]). The FrodoKEM text says "there is still a sizeable gap between small-error LWE parameters that are known to be vulnerable, and those conforming to a worst-case reduction. Most proposed implementations use parameters that lie within this gap" (p.7, [L37]).
- **ML-KEM's security claim does not rest on the reductions.** Kyber's round-3 specification bases its security estimates "on the cost estimates of attacks against the underlying module-learning-with-errors (MLWE) problem" (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 4.2, p.19, [L38]). The MLWE it defines uses the centred binomial distribution B_eta for both s and e (Sec. 4.3, p.19, [L39]), not the Gaussian of LS15. FIPS 203 Sec. 3.2 says the security of ML-KEM "is based on the presumed hardness" of MLWE, whose hardness "is itself based on the presumed hardness of certain computational problems in module lattices" (FIPS 203 p.13, [L40]); it does not claim a proof for its parameters.

Reading for Turing: a lattice layer's security level is an attack-cost estimate (attack-cost-estimation.md), not a theorem. The reductions tell us which problem family is sound; they do not tell us that 1000 dimensions gives any particular number of bits.

## 6. Regev and LPR encryption, with a toy example

**Regev encryption (Regev 2009 Sec. 5, p.31-32, [L41]).** Secret key s uniform in Z_q^n. Public key: m LWE samples (a_i, b_i = <a_i, s> + e_i). To encrypt a bit, choose a random subset S of the m samples and output (sum a_i, sum b_i) for bit 0, or (sum a_i, floor(q/2) + sum b_i) for bit 1. To decrypt (a, b), compute b - <a, s> = (sum of e_i over S) + bit floor(q/2), and output 0 if it is closer to 0 than to floor(q/2). Correctness (Lemma 5.1): if the sum of up to m errors has absolute value below floor(q/2)/2 (about q/4) with probability 1 - delta, decryption errs with probability at most delta. Regev's example setting: q prime between n^2 and 2n^2, m = (1 + eps)(n + 1) log q.

**Lindner-Peikert (LP) encryption (CT-RSA 2011, Sec. 3.1, p.7, [L42]).** A shared uniform matrix A-bar. Key generation: small R1, R2; public key P = R1 - A-bar R2; secret key R2. Encryption with small e1, e2, e3: c1 = e1^T A-bar + e2^T, c2 = e1^T P + e3^T + encode(m), where encode(m) = m floor(q/2). Decryption computes c1 R2 + c2 = e1^T R1 + e2^T R2 + e3^T + encode(m), then decodes: 0 if the value lies in [-floor(q/4), floor(q/4)), else 1, so the tolerated error is t = floor(q/4). In words, decryption works as long as the inner product <e, r> of the sender's noise with the receiver's secret stays below q/4 in absolute value. The ring analogue (LP11 p.8) is the "LPR" scheme; Kyber's design is "the module version of the Ring-LWE LPR encryption scheme" (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 1.5, p.12, [L43]).

**Toy runs (toy_lwe.py, q = 97, so q/4 = 24.25).**

The script also checks the threshold itself: with q = 97 and Regev's rule, bit 0 decodes correctly exactly for noise in [-24, 23] and bit 1 for [-24, 24], so |noise| < floor(q/2)/2 = 24 (Lemma 5.1's condition) suffices for both and nothing beyond 24 works ([C12]). The exact failure probabilities are computed by convolving the exact noise distribution and applying the decoder mod q. The lesson: the decryption noise is a sum of about 2n products of small numbers, so its size grows like sqrt(n) times the error variance; at fixed q, raising n or the error width pushes it past q/4 and decryption starts to fail. Real schemes pick q large enough that the failure probability is tiny and compute it exactly (decryption-failures.md).

## 7. Structured variants: Ring-LWE, Module-LWE, LWR, Module-LWR, NTRU, SIS

Plain LWE needs an n x m random matrix A: for n = 976 and 976 + 8 samples that is about a million entries of 16 bits (FrodoKEM regenerates A from a seed for this reason). Structured variants replace the random matrix by one built from a few polynomials, which saves space and allows fast multiplication. The price is algebraic structure an attacker might use.

**Ring-LWE (LPR 2010; J. ACM version June 25, 2013, [L30]).** Work in R_q = Z_q[x]/(f(x)); LPR's main example is f = x^n + 1 with n a power of 2, and q = 1 mod 2n prime. A sample is (a, b = a s + e) with a, s, e in R_q, e small. One ring sample gives n pseudorandom values in Z_q "rather than just one scalar" and "can replace n samples" of plain LWE, cutting key size by a factor of n; multiplication costs O(n log n) with an FFT/NTT (LPR10 p.3, [L44]). Written out, a s is the product of a structured n x n matrix (negacyclic: each column is the previous one rotated with the wrapped entry negated) with the coefficient vector of s. Hardness: quantum reduction from worst-case approximate SVP on *ideal* lattices (not all lattices). PRS 2017 extends the decision form to "any number field and any modulus" (Peikert-Regev-Stephens-Davidowitz STOC 2017, abstract, [L45]); that matters because ML-KEM's q = 3329 does not satisfy q = 1 mod 2n for n = 256 (section 10).

**Module-LWE (Langlois-Stehle 2015, [L31]).** Take a rank-d module over R_q: the secret is a vector of d ring elements, and A is a d x d (or m x d) matrix of ring elements. As plain LWE this is a (d n)-dimensional problem whose matrix is made of d x d negacyclic n x n blocks (LS15 p.16, figure after Def. 4.6, [L46]). d = 1 is Ring-LWE; n = 1 is plain LWE. ML-KEM uses n = 256 and rank k = 2, 3, 4 (FIPS 203 Table 2, [L50]). Kyber's rationale: Module-LWE gives "somewhat reduced structure compared to Ring-LWE, much better scalability" and, for 256-bit messages, "performance very similar to Ring-LWE-based schemes"; one fixed ring serves all security levels (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 1.5, p.12, [L47]). Hardness: quantum reduction from worst-case problems on module lattices; converse reductions exist, which LS15 point out is not known for Ring-LWE (LS15 abstract, [L33]). Albrecht-Deo (ASIACRYPT 2017, revised 01/2020) reduce MLWE of rank d and modulus q to RLWE with modulus q^d, with a large error increase, so the two are polynomially equivalent in theory (abstract, [L48]); Kyber's specification notes that applying this to Kyber768 gives an RLWE instance with q' = q^3 and error > q^2 times larger, and reads it as a hint that larger module rank may be more secure in concrete terms (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 5.3.1, p.31, [L49]). In the other direction NIST's round-2 report calls RLWE "the most structured" and plain LWE "the least structured", notes an RLWE-to-MLWE reduction by which "the security of NewHope is never better than that of KYBER", and records "a slight but clear preference for KYBER and for low-rank MLWE schemes over RLWE schemes" (NIST IR 8309 Sec. 3.12, PDF p.23-24, [L104]).

Worked example (toy_mlwe.py Part A, [C13]): rank k = 2 over R_17 = Z_17[x]/(x^4 + 1). One MLWE sample b = A s + e with A a 2 x 2 matrix of polynomials unrolls into a plain LWE sample of dimension k n = 8 whose 8 x 8 matrix has only k^2 n = 16 free entries instead of 64. The script prints the matrix and checks that each block is negacyclic.

**LWR (Banerjee-Peikert-Rosen, EUROCRYPT 2012, [L51]).** Replace the random error by deterministic rounding: b = round((p/q) <a, s>) mod p, for moduli q >= p >= 2 (BPR12 Def. 3.1 and eq. 2.1, p.10). The "error" is the rounding error, about q/p in absolute size, error rate about 1/p (p.3). BPR12 Theorem 3.2 (p.11) proves decision-LWR at least as hard as decision-LWE when q >= p B n^omega(1), a super-polynomial modulus; the authors expect LWR to be hard for q/p >= sqrt(n) integer, but that is a conjecture from the state of attacks, not a proof (p.11, [L52]). Later reductions allow polynomial modulus but only for a bounded number of samples: Alwen-Krenn-Pietrzak-Wichs (CRYPTO 2013) and Bogdanov-Guo-Masny-Richelson-Rosen (TCC 2016A), who need the number of samples to be at most O(q/(B p)) (BGMRR16 abstract, [L53]). Toy (lf_structured_toys.py Part B, q = 256, p = 32, [C41]): (q/p) b = <a, s> + err with |err| <= q/(2p) = 4 in 500 samples, so a scaled LWR sample is an LWE sample whose error is a deterministic function of (a, s). **Module-LWR** is the module version; Saber is built on it. Saber's IND-CPA theorem reduces to two decisional Mod-LWR instances (Saber r3 spec Thm 6.1, p.18, [L54]); FireSaber uses l = 4, n = 256, q = 2^13, p = 2^10, T = 2^6, mu = 6 (Saber r3 spec Table 2, p.12, [L55]). Saber was a NIST round-3 finalist and was not selected (status: see nist-pqc-standards.md). Kyber uses rounding (Compress) only as extra noise on top of binomial noise and notes that for Kyber512 the rounding adds 6 bits of core-SVP hardness under a weak LWR assumption (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 1.5, p.13, [L56]).

**NTRU (Hoffstein-Pipher-Silverman, ANTS 1998, [L57]).** Ring R = Z[X]/(X^N - 1), two coprime moduli p (small) and q. Key generation: small f, g; f must be invertible mod q and mod p with inverses Fq, Fp; public key h = Fq * g (mod q), private key f. Encryption: e = p phi * h + m (mod q) with a random small phi. Decryption: a = f * e (mod q) with coefficients centred in (-q/2, q/2), then Fp * a (mod p) = m. It works because f * e = p phi * g + f * m (mod q), and if all coefficients of p phi * g + f * m lie in (-q/2, q/2) the reduction mod q changes nothing, so reducing mod p leaves f * m (HPS98 Sec. 1.2-1.5, p.269-270, [L57]). The hard problem: given h = g / f mod q, find the short (f, g); equivalently, find an unusually short vector in the NTRU lattice {(x, y): y = h x mod q}, a rank-2 module lattice of dimension 2N. HPS98's example sets used N = 107, 167, 503 with q = 64, 128, 256 (HPS98 Sec. 3, p.278, [L58]); their security estimates are 1998 values and are obsolete. Toy (lf_structured_toys.py Part C, N = 17, p = 3, q = 64, [C42]): in 300 encryptions the largest coefficient of p phi*g + f*m is 22 < q/2 = 32, so f*e mod q equals it exactly and every message decrypts; with q = 16 and the same weights, 268 of 300 decryptions fail. The NTRU lattice has dimension 2N = 34 and volume q^N; (f, g) has norm 3.61 against a Gaussian-heuristic 11.29, and LLL finds a lattice vector of norm 3.00 that decrypts 100 of 100 test messages: at toy size the key falls at once. The round-3 NIST NTRU submission uses x^n - 1 with prime n, e.g. ntruhps4096821 (n = 821, q = 4096) and ntruhrss701 (n = 701, q = 8192) (NTRU r3 spec, Sept 30 2020, Sec. 1.6, p.9, [L59]). NTRU Prime uses the field (Z/q)[x]/(x^p - x - 1), p prime, q prime, x^p - x - 1 irreducible mod q ("a prime-degree number field with a large Galois group and an inert modulus, minimizing the number of ring homomorphisms available to the attacker"; NTRU Prime r3 spec, 20201007, Sec. 2 p.24 and p.6, [L60]).

**SIS and Module-SIS.** SIS: given random A in Z_q^(n x m), find a non-zero short z with A z = 0 (mod q); that is, a short vector in Lambda_q^perp(A) (MR09 Sec. 3, p.5-6, [L9]). It is the basis of hash functions and signatures rather than encryption. FIPS 204 describes it the same way (find non-zero t with A t = 0 and small infinity norm) and says ML-DSA's security rests on MLWE and a non-standard variant of Module-SIS called SelfTargetMSIS (FIPS 204 Sec. 3.2, PDF p.19, [L61]). SIS matters for Turing only if the lattice layer ever includes signatures (out of this topic's scope).

## 8. Attacks that exploit algebraic structure

The honest summary from the specifications: for the parameters actually proposed, the best known attacks on Ring-LWE and Module-LWE do not use the structure. Kyber: "The best known attacks against the underlying MLWE problem in Kyber do not make use of the structure in the lattice. We therefore analyze the hardness of the MLWE problem as an LWE problem" (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 5.1, p.25, [L62]). FrodoKEM (which chose plain LWE for caution) agrees that for recommended Ring- and Module-LWE parameters "the current best attacks perform essentially the same as those for plain LWE, apart from some obvious linear-factor (in the ring dimension) savings in time and memory" (FrodoKEM r3 spec Sec. 1.2.1, p.6, [L63]). But structure has broken other problems:

1. **Short generators of principal ideals (Cramer-Ducas-Peikert-Regev, EUROCRYPT 2016, version Feb 25 2016, [L64]).** Given a principal ideal known to have an unusually short generator in a prime-power cyclotomic ring, a quantum polynomial-time (classical 2^(n^(2/3+eps))) algorithm recovers it. This breaks schemes built on that problem (Smart-Vercauteren, the GGH13 multilinear map, Soliloquy). It also gives a quantum polynomial-time algorithm for 2^(O~(sqrt n))-approximate SVP on principal ideal lattices. CDPR16 state these "do not appear to affect" the foundation of Ring-LWE: the first needs an unusually short generator, the second only reaches large approximation factors, and both apply only to principal ideals (p.3-4, [L65]). Later work extended the approximation result to all ideals of cyclotomic rings (FrodoKEM spec refs [34,45,46]); FrodoKEM notes these do not apply "to Ring- or Module-LWE themselves, only Ideal-SVP", and that for n <= 2000 their approximation factors are worse than those reachable by ordinary lattice reduction at level-1 cost (FrodoKEM r3 spec p.6, [L63]). NTRU Prime's specification reads the same history more pessimistically, as a reason to avoid cyclotomic rings (NTRU Prime r3 spec p.6, [L66]).
2. **Overstretched NTRU (Albrecht-Bai-Ducas 2016; Kirchner-Fouque 2017; Ducas-van Woerden 2021).** When q is large compared with the size of f and g, the NTRU lattice contains an unusually dense sublattice, and lattice reduction finds it much sooner than LWE-style analysis predicts. ABD16 used subfields to break NTRU-based FHE (LTV, YASHE) in sub-exponential time and GGH-like multilinear maps; it explicitly "does not apply for small moduli and hence NTRUEncrypt" (ABD16 abstract, [L67]); ABD16 Table 7 computed that NTRU-743 (q = 2048) is "perfectly immune" (p.21, [L68]). Kirchner-Fouque showed the effect needs no algebraic structure (it is geometric) and works "in any ring for large q, such as the NTRU Prime ring"; they write that these attacks "do not endanger the security of the NTRUEncrypt" (KF17 abstract, [L69]) and conclude that "the shortest vector problem over module lattices seems strictly easier than the bounded distance decoding", recommending Ring-LWE over NTRU where the cost is small (KF17 Sec. 7, p.21, [L70]). Ducas-van Woerden (ASIACRYPT 2021) located the "fatigue point" for ternary NTRU at q = n^(2.484+o(1)), concretely q ~ 0.004 n^2.484 for n > 100, and state that this "does not contradict the concrete security of the NTRU candidate"; the NTRU KEM uses q = O(n), "with a hidden constant between 4 and 5 in practice" (DvW21 abstract and p.2-3, [L71]). For n = 821 the fatigue point is about 0.004 x 821^2.484, roughly 69,000, while ntruhps4096821 uses q = 4096 (COMPUTED, [C14]). They also note that the n rotations of the NTRU key give "a minor security degradation compared to (Ring)-LWE already in the standard regime" (DvW21 footnote 3, p.2, [L72]).
3. **Weak Ring-LWE instantiations.** Some non-standard Ring-LWE instances (special number fields, narrow spherical errors in the "non-dual" form) were broken. Peikert (SCN 2016) shows that in all cases the cause was an error distribution "insufficiently 'well spread' relative to the ring", and that instantiations meeting the worst-case-hardness hypotheses are immune to broad generalisations of those attacks (Peikert 2016 abstract, [L73]). FrodoKEM's footnote 3 says the same (p.6, [L63]).
4. **Albrecht-Deo** (above) is a reduction, not an attack; for Kyber it leads to much harder RLWE instances.

**Why these do not reach Kyber or NTRU KEM parameters.** CDPR-type results need principal ideals with a planted short generator or only reach 2^(O~(sqrt n)) approximations, which at n <= 2000 are weaker than ordinary BKZ ([L63], [L65]). Overstretched-NTRU attacks need q far above the NTRU KEM's q = O(n) ([L71], [C14]). Module-LWE with Gaussian-like errors wide enough for the ring is outside the weak-instance class ([L73]). The residual risk is the unknown: LS15 call the easiness of GapSVP on ideal lattices "a warning of potential risk" (LS15 p.4, [L74]), and NTRU Prime's authors list structure-exploiting algorithms among "serious risks of further advances" (NTRU Prime r3 spec p.6, [L66]).

## 9. Error distributions

Three families are in use. All checks below are in `lf_error_distributions.py` ([C20]-[C25]).

1. **Discrete or rounded Gaussian.** The theory (Regev, LPR) is stated for continuous Gaussians, which "trivially apply to rounded Gaussian" (NewHope 2016 Sec. 4, [L80]). Sampling a Gaussian exactly is slow and hard to do in constant time (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 1.5, p.13, [L81]). FrodoKEM samples a fixed 16-bit table that approximates the rounded Gaussian: Frodo-976 uses sigma = 2.3, support [-10, 10], probabilities in units of 2^-16 given in FrodoKEM r3 spec Table 3 (p.25; identical in the 2025 proposal Table A.3), chosen to minimise the Renyi divergence from the rounded Gaussian (r3 spec p.23, [L82]). Recomputed: the table sums to exactly 2^16; its standard deviation is 2.3178, matching the rounded Gaussian's own 2.3180 (= sqrt(2.3^2 + 1/12), because rounding adds variance about 1/12); the sampling table T_chi (2025 Table A.4) is its cumulative form; and D_500(table || rounded Gaussian) = 1.395e-5 against the printed 0.140e-4. The same holds for Frodo-640 (3.237e-5 vs 0.324e-4) and Frodo-1344 (2.636e-5 vs 0.264e-4) ([C21]).
2. **Centred binomial CBD(eta).** x - y with x, y each a sum of eta fair bits (FIPS 203 Algorithm 8, PDF p.32, [L83]). Exact pmf for eta = 2: 1/16, 1/4, 3/8, 1/4, 1/16 on -2..2; variance eta/2 ([C20]). ML-KEM uses eta = 2 (std 1) and, for ML-KEM-512's eta1, eta = 3 (std 1.2247). NewHope introduced it; its safeguard theorem bounds the loss against the rounded Gaussian of the same std by a Renyi-divergence argument: R_9(psi_16 || xi) ~ 1.00063, and R_9^(5n) <= 2^6 for n = 1024 (NewHope 2016 Thm 4.1 and App. B, [L84]). Recomputed: 1.0006357 and 25.89 ([C22]). The authors then "simply ignore the loss factor ... since the best-known attacks against LWE do not exploit the structure of the error distribution, and seem to depend only on the standard deviation of the error (except in extreme cases)" (App. B, [L84]). Kyber repeats this: attack performance "does not depend on the exact distribution of noise, but rather on the standard deviation (and potentially the entropy)" (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 1.5, p.13, [L81]).
3. **Rounding (LWR).** No sampled error: the error is what rounding from q down to p throws away. From q = 2^13 to p = 2^10 (Saber), the error is uniform over q/p = 8 consecutive integers with variance 63/12 = 5.25, std 2.29 ([C23]). Saber's secrets use beta_mu, a centred binomial with pmf mu!/((mu/2 + x)!(mu/2 - x)!) 2^-mu on [-mu/2, mu/2], variance mu/4 (Saber r3 spec Sec. 2.1, [L85]; [C24]).

**Width versus security versus failure.** A wider error (larger std relative to q) makes the planted short vector (s, e, 1) longer, so the attacker needs a larger BKZ block size: security rises. The same width enters the decryption noise, which is a sum of about 2 k n products of small numbers, so its std grows like sqrt(2 k n) times the product of the widths: failure probability rises (toy_lwe.py Parts B-D show this at q = 97). Designers balance the two by choosing q: FrodoKEM uses q = 2^16 with std 2.3; ML-KEM uses q = 3329 with std 1 and compresses ciphertexts, which adds rounding noise. Exact failure probabilities are decryption-failures.md's topic.

**Every deployed distribution is narrower than the quantum reductions require.** Those need std >= c sqrt(n) with c > 1/(2 pi) ([L35]). At n = 1024 that is about 5.09; ML-KEM's std is 1.0, FrodoKEM-976's 2.3, NewHope1024's 2.0 ([C25]). This is the concrete form of "parameters lie within the gap" ([L37]).

**Very narrow errors are dangerous.** LWE with errors bounded by a constant is solvable in polynomial time given enough samples (Arora-Ge 2011, cited by FrodoKEM r3 spec p.7, [L86]). Real schemes limit samples: ML-KEM exposes (k + 1) n per key ([L23]), FrodoKEM "far less than the number required by known attacks" ([L36]).

## 10. The NTT and the choice n = 256, q = 3329

The number-theoretic transform (NTT) is a discrete Fourier transform over Z_q. It turns multiplication in R_q = Z_q[X]/(X^n + 1) into many small independent multiplications, by the Chinese remainder theorem: if X^n + 1 factors mod q into pieces, a polynomial is represented by its remainders modulo each piece. Remainders multiply independently.

- **When does X^n + 1 split?** Its roots are the primitive 2n-th roots of unity. Over a prime q it splits into n/d irreducible factors of degree d, where d is the order of q modulo 2n (finite-field fact; checked in [C30]). If 2n divides q - 1, then d = 1: n linear factors, a complete NTT (NewHope: q = 12289, n = 1024, and 12289 is the smallest prime = 1 mod 2048, NewHope r2 spec Sec. 2, p.17, [L87]; [C31]). If only n divides q - 1, d = 2: n/2 quadratic factors, an "incomplete" NTT.
- **ML-KEM.** q = 3329 = 2^8 13 + 1; there are 128 primitive 256-th roots of unity and none of order 512; zeta = 17 is a primitive 256-th root with zeta^128 = -1; X^256 + 1 = prod_{i=0}^{127} (X^2 - zeta^(2 BitRev7(i) + 1)); so R_q is isomorphic to T_q, a direct sum of 128 quadratic extension fields (FIPS 203 Sec. 4.3, printed p.24-25, [L88]). Recomputed: the product identity holds, every constant zeta^(2 BitRev7(i)+1) is a quadratic non-residue (so each quadratic factor is irreducible), and an implementation of FIPS 203 Algorithms 9-12 written from the text agrees with schoolbook multiplication on 20 random pairs ([C30]).
- **Why these numbers.** n = 256 "because the goal is to encapsulate keys with 256 bits of entropy"; q is "a small prime satisfying n | (q - 1)"; the smaller such primes 257 and 769 cannot reach the negligible failure probability CCA security needs, so q = 3329 (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 1.4, p.11, [L89]). Recomputed: 257, 769 and 3329 are exactly the primes = 1 mod 256 up to 3329 ([C32]). Security levels then scale only through the module rank k, with n and q fixed; "Larger values of n would reduce the capability to easily scale security via parameter k" ([L89]).
- **Implications for other dimensions.** With q = 3329, X^512 + 1 splits into 128 factors of degree 4 and X^1024 + 1 into 128 factors of degree 8 ([C31]), so a larger ring degree with the same q gives a less efficient NTT; a complete NTT at n = 1024 needs q = 1 mod 2048 (12289, 18433, ...). Power-of-two moduli (Saber, NTRU, FrodoKEM) have no NTT at all (x^8 + 1 = (x + 1)^8 mod 2, [C31]); Saber and NTRU use Toom-Cook/Karatsuba-style multiplication and FrodoKEM plain matrix products. NTRU Prime chooses an *inert* modulus on purpose: x^p - x - 1 stays irreducible mod q, so there is no NTT factorisation. Recomputed with Rabin's test: x^761 - x - 1 is irreducible mod 4591 and x^1013 - x - 1 is irreducible mod 7177 ([C33]).
- **Security relevance.** The NTT is a representation, not a weakening, for MLWE as analysed today: FIPS 203 even samples A directly in NTT form. PRS17 removed the old "q = 1 mod 2n" condition from the decision-RLWE reduction ([L45]), so q = 3329's partial splitting is covered in theory. What the NTT does add is implementation risk (its intermediate values leak through side channels; see side-channels-faults-and-ct-verification.md).

## 11. What "dimension" means, and real ~1000-dimension instances

"About 1000 dimensions" can mean four different numbers:

1. **Plain-LWE secret dimension n.** FrodoKEM-976: n = 976.
2. **Module-LWE: rank k times ring degree n.** ML-KEM-1024: k n = 4 x 256 = 1024 (LS15: "the corresponding module lattice has dimension N = nd", p.8, [L31]).
3. **Ring degree for NTRU / Ring-LWE.** NewHope1024: n = 1024; ntruhps4096821: n = 821; sntrup1013: p = 1013. The NTRU lattice itself has dimension 2n.
4. **The dimension of the lattice the attacker reduces.** The primal attack embeds the LWE instance in {x in Z^(m + kn + 1): (A | I_m | -b) x = 0 mod q}, dimension d = m + kn + 1, volume q^m, with the unique short vector (s, e, 1) of norm about sigma sqrt(kn + m) (Kyber r3 spec Sec. 5.1.2, p.26, [L90]; FrodoKEM r3 spec Sec. 5.2.2, p.42, same form with d = m + n + 1, [L91]). The attacker picks m to minimise cost. For ML-KEM-1024 the core-SVP optimum is d = 1885 with BKZ block size 878 (Kyber r3 spec Table 4, p.21, [L92]); a pure-GSA recomputation gives b = 874, d = 1867 ([C34]; the official script also simulates the unreduced q-vectors). For FrodoKEM-976 the refined estimate uses d = 1969, b = 724 (FrodoKEM r3 spec Table 11, PDF p.44, [L93]); the core-SVP primal cost 216.0 (Table 10) is reproduced to 0.2 bit by pure GSA with b = 707 at d = 1949 ([C35]). The BKZ block size, not n, sets the cost.

## What this means for Turing

Context: Turing's current design uses X-Wing (X25519 + ML-KEM-768) through vetted code and says "Only the data cipher is ours" (docs/03-design-spec.md, lines 11-17). The owner now asks for a lattice layer at about 1000 dimensions "with a twist". From the foundations side:

1. **Say which dimension is meant.** "1000 dimensions" should be pinned to one of: ML-KEM-1024 (MLWE, k n = 1024), FrodoKEM-976 (LWE, n = 976), or a ring degree near 1000 (NewHope1024, sntrup1013). Moving from ML-KEM-768 to ML-KEM-1024 is the zero-invention way to reach ~1000 dimensions: it is in FIPS 203, it raises the claimed category from 3 to 5 ([L102]), and the primal attack lattice grows from d = 1419 (b = 626) to d = 1885 (b = 878) ([L50], [L92]). FIPS 203 recommends ML-KEM-768 as the default but also says that "when initially establishing cryptographic protections for data, the strongest possible parameter set should be used" ([L102]). Evidence: FIPS 203 Table 2; Kyber r3 Table 4.
2. **Security must be argued by attack estimates, not by the reductions.** Any parameter set Turing uses needs a published or reproduced estimate (attack-cost-estimation.md) and an exact decryption-failure computation (decryption-failures.md). A new parameter set "with a worst-case reduction" is not thereby secure: the reductions do not cover deployed widths ([C25]) and are non-tight ([L34]).
3. **Twists that the literature supports (each still needs sibling-topic work):**
   - *Diversity of assumptions, not a new assumption.* Pair a structured KEM (ML-KEM-1024, MLWE) with an unstructured one (FrodoKEM-976, plain LWE) under a vetted combiner. A future attack that uses ring or module structure would then not break both. Evidence: the reductions for plain LWE start from all lattices, those for Module-LWE only from module lattices ([L33]); FrodoKEM's design rationale is exactly this caution ([L32], [L63]); NIST's reason for dropping FrodoKEM was performance ([L100]). Costs: 15.6 KB public key and 15.8 KB ciphertext for FrodoKEM-976 ([L96]); FrodoKEM is not a NIST standard (status: nist-pqc-standards.md; combiners: hybrid-combiners.md). This is the strongest "harder to break" option from this topic's evidence, because it removes a single point of failure without inventing anything.
   - *Non-cyclotomic ring.* NTRU Prime's x^p - x - 1 with an inert prime modulus avoids subfields and NTT-style factorisations by design ([L60], [C33]). Evidence for a benefit is argumentative (fewer homomorphisms for an attacker, [L66]); no attack on cyclotomic Module-LWE is known that it would stop ([L62]). NIST removed NTRU Prime ([L100]). Moderate support, significant cost (no NTT, custom implementation).
   - *Larger module rank.* ML-KEM scales security through k; Albrecht-Deo's reduction hints that larger rank makes structure harder to exploit ([L49]). But k > 4 has no official parameter set: failure probability and attack cost would have to be recomputed and the implementation would leave FIPS 203. Treat as an option only with sibling estimates.
4. **Twists the evidence warns against:**
   - Narrower error than analysed, or "rounding only" with many samples: small errors with many samples are breakable (Arora-Ge, [L86]); LWR proofs need bounded samples ([L53]).
   - A home-made ring, modulus or error shape without an estimate: the broken Ring-LWE instances were exactly non-standard rings with too-narrow errors ([L73]).
   - NTRU with a large modulus (overstretched): q must stay far below 0.004 n^2.484 ([L71], [C14]).
   - Believing that a larger n alone adds security: security follows the block size b needed, which depends on n, q and the error width together ([L90], [C34]-[C35]). FrodoKEM-976 (n = 976, category 3 target) needs a core-SVP block size of about 707 (refined 724), ML-KEM-1024 (k n = 1024, category 5) 878 (refined 894), despite their similar "dimension" ([L92], [L93], [C35]).
   - Secret or obscure parameters: they add no analysable security and break interoperability; every analysed scheme publishes all parameters.
5. **Implementation constraints from the maths.** Choosing q decides the multiplication algorithm: q = 3329 with n = 256 gives the FIPS 203 incomplete NTT; other (n, q) pairs change it ([C31]). Any Turing implementation must be tested against FIPS 203's algorithms; lf_ntt_and_dimensions.py shows that a from-text implementation of Algorithms 9-12 can be checked against schoolbook multiplication, which is the kind of oracle testing the testing-ci-cd and rust-pqc-implementations topics should build on. Side-channel risks of the NTT and of samplers belong to side-channels-faults-and-ct-verification.md.
6. **Learning path for the owner.** The scripts form a sequence: lattice_basics.py (bases, determinants, Gram-Schmidt, Minkowski, Gaussian heuristic, LLL, the primal attack at toy size), toy_lwe.py (why noise, Regev and LP encryption, the q/4 rule, failures), toy_mlwe.py (one Module-LWE sample, its LWE unrolling, NTTs, Kyber-shaped encryption), lf_structured_toys.py (normal form, LWR, NTRU and a toy NTRU key recovery), lf_error_distributions.py (CBD, FrodoKEM tables, Renyi arguments, LWR rounding), lf_ntt_and_dimensions.py (ML-KEM's NTT, other moduli, NTRU Prime fields, attack-lattice dimensions, NTRU fatigue).

## Open questions

1. **Exact reproduction of the official primal numbers.** The pure-GSA model here gives b = 874 / d = 1867 for ML-KEM-1024 against the specification's 878 / 1885 ([C34]). The difference is expected (the official script simulates the q-vector region and scans a grid), but a reproduction with the official script is attack-cost-estimation.md's job.
2. **Current status of FrodoKEM outside NIST.** The team's proposal (revision 2025-09-29) calls itself a "Preliminary Standardization Proposal"; which body it targets and where that process stands on 2026-09-27 was not checked here (nist-pqc-standards.md / pq-families-for-diversity.md).
3. **ML-KEM with k > 4.** No primary source found in this run that analyses or standardises rank 5 or more; failure probability and cost would need new computation.
4. **Does the NTRU fatigue estimate apply to NTRU Prime and to ntru-hrss's non-fixed-weight secrets?** DvW21 state it for ternary NTRU; the extrapolation in [C14] is ours.
5. **NTRU Prime and NewHope byte sizes** were not transcribed (the NTRU Prime size table's column order did not survive text extraction); read NTRU Prime r3 spec Table 1 and NewHope r2 spec Table 3 by page image if needed.
6. **Arora-Ge (2011)** is cited here through FrodoKEM's specification; the primary paper (research/papers/2011-arora-ge-new-algorithms-learning-with-errors.pdf) was not re-read in this run.
7. **Superscripts in Saber's and Kyber's tables** were read from flattened text (e.g. "2−165", "213"); Saber's parameter line was confirmed with pdfspans.py, the failure and core-SVP columns were not individually span-checked.
8. **Module-LWR proofs for Saber's exact parameters** (bounded samples, q/p = 8): whether BGMRR16's sample bound O(q/(B p)) covers Saber's sample count was not worked out.
