# From IND-CPA lattice encryption to a robust IND-CCA KEM

Status: complete after a second (continuation) pass on 2026-09-27, which re-ran every script, corrected one misread formula (C9), closed seven of the ten earlier open questions and added FO13, Barbosa-Hülsing, Hövelmanns-Kudinov, Starfighters, verifiable decapsulation, the HQC salt-binding attack and Bernstein's multi-ciphertext dissent. Open items are listed at the end. Date checked: 2026-09-27. Notation: #M (or |M| in prose) is the number of possible FO messages.

This file covers how a lattice public-key encryption scheme that is only IND-CPA secure becomes a robust IND-CCA key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM): the Fujisaki-Okamoto (FO) transform family, implicit versus explicit rejection, proofs in the quantum random oracle model (QROM), the exact FO variants in ML-KEM (FIPS 203) and FrodoKEM, key-reuse attacks on IND-CPA-only schemes, KEM binding properties (including C2PRI for X-Wing-style combiners and robustness/anonymity for KEM-DEM files), randomness failures and hedging, multi-target security, input validation, and tests that catch a missing check. It ends with a sourced checklist a custom lattice KEM for Turing must satisfy. Scripts are in research/scripts/pq/cca_*.py; each prints PASS/FAIL lines and was re-run on 2026-09-27.

## Key findings

1. **The lattice part is only IND-CPA; the FO wrapper makes it IND-CCA.** FIPS 203 forbids using K-PKE alone [L1]. Without FO, a reused lattice key falls to a match/mismatch oracle: Fluhrer estimated about 4,000 queries for a 1024-dimension ring-LWE key [K3]; Qin et al. give lower bounds of 1216 / 1632 / 2176 queries for Kyber512/768/1024 [K8]. (Section 2)
2. **ML-KEM's transform = HHK17 FO with implicit rejection, plus H(ek) in the coin/key hash** (FIPS 203 Alg. 17-18) [L2, L11]. Relative to round-3 Kyber, FIPS 203 dropped H(c) from the key and dropped m ← H(m); final FIPS 203 added G(d ‖ k) domain separation over the ipd [L4, L5, L9]. (Section 4)
3. **Proof status.** ROM: tight from IND-CPA [C5]. QROM: every generic bound loses at least a square root, with δ entering multiplied by q or q² [C9, C12, C14]; HHM22 made the failure loss linear in q_G and covers explicit rejection [C20]. Worked example: HHK17's QROM bound says nothing beyond about 2^41 queries even for a perfect lattice, HHM22's parameter-free terms stay below 1 up to about 2^124 [C23]. So QROM proofs are qualitative evidence; parameters come from cryptanalysis [L22]. The proofs cover only the exact transform: the original FO proof, HHK17's U_m theorems and Duman et al.'s theorems all needed published fixes [C26, C7, M14], and round-3 Kyber's H(c) variant could not be justified at levels III and V by its proven bound [C22]. (Section 3)
4. **Rejection must be uniform, constant-time and silent, and a file format will make it explicit anyway.** The rejection key must use a secret z and the full ciphertext [B5]. It must be computed every time, and the flag never exposed [L12, L13]; FO's own 2013 paper already required one error symbol and constant-time rejection after timing attacks on EPOC [C28], and a timing leak enabled key recovery on FrodoKEM [F2]. In file encryption a wrong KEM key makes the authenticated decryption fail visibly, so Turing should rely on explicit-rejection security, which Hövelmanns-Kudinov show is as strong as implicit rejection in the ROM up to a rare edge case [C24]. (Sections 3-5)
5. **FrodoKEM now has two variants.** eFrodoKEM = round 3, for at most "e.g., 2^8" ciphertexts per key. Salted FrodoKEM puts a 2κ-bit public salt into the coins and the key and uses a 2κ-bit coin seed (256/384/512 bits) [F3-F7]. The ISO proposal was last revised 2025-09-29. (Section 5)
6. **Multi-target security needs more than IND-CCA.** FO makes encryption deterministic, so many ciphertexts under one key allow collision search in the message space: about 2^88 work against FrodoKEM-640 with 2^40 ciphertexts [M5]. The salted FO has a tight classical-ROM proof (GHS, IACR CiC 2026) [M6]. GHS show unsalted ML-KEM-1024 reaches only 192-bit multi-target security in the ROM [M8]. Separately, Bernstein argues heuristically that the lattice problem itself degrades with many ciphertexts; GHS agree that the earlier claim of no degradation was false and leave it unbounded [M10, M12-M14]. (Section 8)
7. **Binding.** IND-CCA does not stop re-encapsulation / unknown-key-share attacks [B1]. ML-KEM is LEAK-binding but not MAL-BIND-K-CT, MAL-BIND-K-PK or MAL-BIND-K,CT-PK [B8, B14]. Checking the stored hash fixes K-CT [B11]. K-PK needs a single seed from which z is derived [B11, B12]. FIPS 203's two-part (d, z) seed and RFC 9935's 64-byte seed are not enough [R14]. KSW's FO_M (hash hpk into the rejection key) achieves all MAL notions [B15]. (Section 6)
8. **A salt must be hashed into both keys.** Round-4 HQC put its salt in the ciphertext and the coins but not the key hash, and so lost 10 of 12 binding notions, including HON-BIND-K-CT with no secret needed; hashing the whole ciphertext restores FO's results [B25-B27]. Reproduced on a toy salted FO over ML-KEM's K-PKE [B28]. (Section 6)
9. **X-Wing-style combiners need C2PRI from the lattice KEM.** Starfighters (IEEE S&P 2026) prove C2PRI for every FO variant on a rigid PKE, salted FO included; ML-KEM's bound is (q + 3)/2^256 classically [B21-B24]. A custom Turing KEM inside such a combiner must be one of these covered variants. (Section 6)
10. **Randomness.** K and the whole key pair are deterministic functions of m and d, so their entropy caps security [R1, R2]. Demonstrated: a 12-bit-state RNG gives up K from (ek, c) after 3,002 Encaps calls, hashing the RNG output first does not help, and hedging with a secret stops it [R17]. FIPS 203 requires an approved RBG of ≥ 128/192/256-bit strength and fails closed [L15, R6]. Hedging (Bellare 2009; RFC 8937) is the published way to survive a weak RNG [R9, R10]. (Section 7)
11. **Input checks catch real bugs, and conformance vectors catch missing checks.** The FIPS 203 modulus, length and hash checks are mandatory [L17, L18]. The reference implementation passed NIST ACVP, CCTV (including all three accumulated 10,000-test hashes, which target the FIPS 203 ipd [V4, V5]) and Wycheproof vectors. Four injected bugs (no modulus check, no hash check, strcmp comparison, no FO comparison) were each caught [V2, V3]. But a skipped FO check passes ordinary round-trip tests: HQC's reference code accepted malformed ciphertexts for 19 months [V6]. (Section 9)
12. **Verifiable decapsulation is a proven way to make that bug visible.** FO_C (CRYPTO 2025) feeds a short confirmation code from re-encryption into the key, so a decapsulator that skips re-encryption fails basic tests; for ML-KEM the cost is at most 3.4% and K stops being byte-compatible with ML-KEM [V6, V7]. (Section 9)
13. **Evidence-backed "twists" for Turing:** salted FO with the salt in both key hashes [M6-M8, B27], FO_M rejection [B15], confirmation codes (FO_C) [V6], single-seed keys [B11], hedged randomness [R9, R10, R17], and a final KDF over (K, c, ek) [B18]. **Risky twists:** any unproven FO variant, combining two proven variants without a proof of the combination, a Turing-cipher-based random oracle, observable rejection, IND-CPA with reused keys, and parameter changes without recomputing δ [C7, C22, D1, F2, K8]. (What this means for Turing)

## 1. Vocabulary: PKE, KEM, IND-CPA, IND-CCA

- **PKE (public-key encryption).** KeyGen gives (pk, sk). Enc(pk, m; r) uses random coins r. Dec(sk, c) returns m or a failure symbol ⊥.
- **KEM (key-encapsulation mechanism).** KeyGen gives (ek, dk). Encaps(ek) returns (K, c): a fresh random key K and a ciphertext c that carries it. Decaps(dk, c) returns K. A KEM moves a key, not a chosen message; data is then encrypted with a symmetric cipher under K (the "KEM-DEM" pattern) [HHK17 §1, p. 3].
- **Correctness error δ.** The chance that Decaps does not return the key Encaps produced, even with honest inputs. Lattice schemes have δ > 0 because of noise. HHK17 give guidance: "for κ bits security", the PKE needs "a correctness error of 2^-κ" [C5].
- **OW-CPA (one-wayness).** Given pk and an encryption of a random message, the attacker cannot recover the message.
- **IND-CPA.** Given pk, the attacker cannot tell which of two chosen messages was encrypted. The attacker never sees a decryption.
- **IND-CCA (also IND-CCA2).** Same game, but the attacker may ask for decryptions (decapsulations) of any ciphertext except the challenge. This models a server that decrypts whatever it receives and whose behaviour (or error messages, or timing) leaks the result.
- **OW-PCA.** One-wayness when the attacker also has a plaintext-checking oracle Pco(c, m) that says whether c decrypts to m [HHK17 §1, p. 2]. This oracle is exactly what a real "key mismatch" gives an attacker (Section 2).
- **ROM / QROM.** Proofs model hash functions as random functions (random oracle model, ROM). Against a quantum attacker the attacker may evaluate the hash on a superposition of inputs (quantum random oracle model, QROM) [BHHHP19 §1, p. 2]. Proof techniques that "record" the attacker's hash queries do not work directly in the QROM, which is why QROM bounds are looser (Section 3.3).

The recurring lesson of this file: the lattice part of a KEM is only IND-CPA. Everything that makes it safe against active attackers is in the symmetric "wrapper" (hashes, re-encryption, rejection, domain separation, input checks). Mistakes in the wrapper are as fatal as a weak lattice.

## 2. Why IND-CPA with a reused key fails: key-mismatch and reaction attacks

**The attack model.** A server keeps one lattice key pair and answers many clients. An attacker sends crafted ciphertexts and watches only whether the key the server derived equals the attacker's guess. In practice this bit leaks through ordinary behaviour: if the attacker encrypts a message under its guessed key and the server answers sensibly, the guess matched [Fluhrer 2016, §4, p. 3-4] [K1]. This is a plaintext-checking oracle.

**Fluhrer 2016 (ePrint 2016/085).** For ring-LWE key exchange where Alice reuses her key share, Eve crafts her share so that one coefficient of Alice's computed value sits next to a rounding boundary. The match/mismatch bit then reveals the sign of δ·j + k·S[i] for small integers she chooses, and binary search recovers each secret coefficient "with a handful of probes" [K2]. For ring size N = 1024 and a Gaussian secret with standard deviation about 3, Fluhrer estimates "perhaps 4,000 queries" for the whole key [K3]. His conclusion: these protocols "cannot be used as a drop in replacement for designs which use Diffie-Hellman static key shares", and he expects the result to "extend to similar LWE-based protocols" [K4].

**Follow-ups.**
- Ding, Alsayigh, RV, Fluhrer, Lin (2016): the reconciliation "signal" itself leaks the secret of a reused key [K5].
- Bauer, Gilbert, Renault, Rossi (ANSSI; CT-RSA 2019 per ePrint 2019/075): for NewHope, "several thousands of queries are sufficient to recover the full private key"; for the CPA version key reuse "should be strictly avoided, even for an extremely short duration"; for the CCA version the attack points to "critical steps inside the CCA transform that should be carefully protected against faults or side channels" [K6].
- Băetu, Durak, Huguenin-Dumittan, Talayhan, Vaudenay (EUROCRYPT 2019): key recovery against the weak (CPA) versions of nine NIST submissions with a plaintext-checking oracle, and a quantum key recovery if the attacker gets quantum access to a decryption oracle [K7].
- Qin, Cheng, Zhang, Pan, Hu, Ding (ASIACRYPT 2021): a lower bound on the average number of mismatch queries equals a Huffman-code (entropy) computation over the secret-coefficient distribution. Their Table 1 bounds: Kyber512 1216, Kyber768 1632, Kyber1024 2176, Frodo640 18,227, Frodo976 25,796, Frodo1344 27,973 queries; their improved practical attacks (Table 6) need 1312 / 1774 / 2365 for Kyber and 18,329 / 26,000 / 29,353 for Frodo (theory column) [K8, K9]. They also note the same methods cut the traces needed by side-channel attacks on CCA-secure Kyber512 "from 2560 to 1183" [K10]. The earlier-run script `cca_kem_property_tests.py` reproduces the Kyber bounds as n·k × (average Huffman code length of the centred binomial distribution) [K11].

**What this means.** A few thousand queries are cheap. Any design where a long-term lattice secret key decrypts attacker-chosen ciphertexts must be IND-CCA, i.e. must use an FO-style transform, and must not leak the FO check result through side channels (the CCA versions are attacked the same way through timing, power or faults: side-channel topic). This file does not include an attack demonstration; the query counts above come from the published papers. The defensive side is tested instead: the FO re-encryption check must reject every ciphertext that is not an honest encryption, and the ACVP "modified ciphertext" vectors plus a negative control (FO check disabled) show that a test suite catches a missing check (Section 9).

## 3. The Fujisaki-Okamoto family

### 3.1 The idea in two steps (HHK17)

Hofheinz, Hövelmanns and Kiltz (TCC 2017, ePrint 2017/604, version of 2 Nov 2021) split FO into two independent steps [C1]:

1. **T ("derandomise + re-encrypt").** Make encryption deterministic: Enc1(pk, m) = Enc(pk, m; G(m)), the coins are a hash of the message. Decryption decrypts to m' and then re-encrypts: if Enc(pk, m'; G(m')) ≠ c it rejects. Re-encryption is what kills crafted ciphertexts: a ciphertext the attacker built by hand is almost never exactly the encryption of what it decrypts to. T turns OW-CPA into OW-PCA (a plaintext-checking oracle no longer helps), tightly from IND-CPA in the ROM [C2].
2. **U ("hash to a key").** Encaps picks random m, sets c = Enc1(pk, m), K = H(m, c) (or K = H(m) in the "_m" variants). Decapsulation of an invalid c either returns ⊥ (**explicit rejection**, U^⊥) or returns a pseudorandom key H(s, c) from a secret seed s kept in sk (**implicit rejection**, U^⊥̸) [C3]. Implicit rejection in FO was first introduced by Persichetti (2012) per HHK17 [C3].

The combined transforms: FO^⊥̸ = U^⊥̸ ∘ T (implicit), FO^⊥ = U^⊥ ∘ T (explicit), FO^⊥̸_m and FO^⊥_m (K = H(m)), and QFO^⊥_m, which adds a hash d = H'(m) to the ciphertext for the QROM proof [C4]. The explicit-reject variants need the PKE to be γ-spread (ciphertexts have min-entropy γ); the implicit ones do not [C4]. FO^⊥_m "essentially recovers a KEM variant" of the original transform [C4].

**The original transform (Fujisaki-Okamoto, CRYPTO 1999; full version J. Cryptology 26:80-101, 2013).** It encrypts a message m as E_pk^asy(σ; H(σ, c)) ‖ E^sy_G(σ)(m) with c = E^sy_G(σ)(m): a random σ is public-key encrypted with coins derived from σ and the symmetric part, and the symmetric key is G(σ) [C26]. Decryption recovers σ, recomputes the coins and re-encrypts; if the result differs from the received value it outputs an error [C26]. Theorem 6.1 bounds the IND-CCA advantage by 2·q_hash·ε_asy + ε_sy + 2·q_dec·2^-γ, so the reduction from one-wayness loses a factor q_hash [C27]. Three details matter for lattices and for Turing:
- The paper assumes a **perfectly correct** asymmetric scheme: "we always have D_sk(E_pk(x)) = x" [C27]. Lattice schemes are not perfectly correct, which is why HHK17's analysis with a correctness error δ was needed.
- §4.1 warns that the error symbol for the two rejection steps "must be the same"; otherwise decryption "might reveal crucial information". For EPOC this was exploited, including a timing attack that detected in which step a ciphertext was rejected, and the remedy was to always run the re-encryption step with almost the same time [C28]. This is the 1999-2013 form of this file's rule "rejection must be uniform, constant-time and silent".
- The JoC version is described as "the full version of the paper [18] by fixing bugs and providing a clean, formal proof" [C26]: the founding FO proof itself needed repair.

HHK17 errata (first page of the 2021 version): the theorems for U^⊥_m and U^⊥̸_m were wrong as first published; Bernstein and Persichetti observed this, and the fix is to require the deterministic PKE to be **rigid**: Enc(pk, Dec(sk, c)) = c unless decryption fails. T provides rigidity automatically [C7]. This is an example of a published, peer-reviewed FO proof needing repair years later; a custom variant would have no such scrutiny.

### 3.2 ROM bounds and what they ask of parameters

HHK17 §3.3 (PDF p. 22) gives concrete ROM bounds. For the implicit-rejection KEM from an IND-CPA PKE:

Adv^IND-CCA ≤ q_RO·δ + 3·q_RO/|M| + 3·Adv^IND-CPA

and from OW-CPA the last term becomes 2·q_RO·Adv^OW-CPA (non-tight). For κ-bit security they require δ ≤ 2^-κ, γ ≥ κ, and |M| ≥ 2^κ [C5]. In words: the message space must have at least 2^κ elements, and the failure rate must be at most 2^-κ, because an attacker making q_RO hash queries gets q_RO chances to hit a failing message.

### 3.3 Quantum random oracle proofs and their tightness

- HHK17 prove T and QU_m in the QROM but "all our reductions in the quantum random oracle model are non-tight" [C8]. Their QROM bound for QKEM_m (PDF p. 34 table, checked on the rendered page) is 8·q_RO·( sqrt(q_RO²·δ + q_RO·sqrt(Adv^OW-CPA)) + q_RO·δ ). The OW-CPA advantage sits under a fourth root, and the whole expression is multiplied by 8·q_RO [C9]. Worked example (Section 3.4): at q_RO = 2^64 this bound says nothing for any scheme with a 256-bit message space.
- Saito-Xagawa-Yamakawa (EUROCRYPT 2018): a tight QROM reduction for deterministic PKE with a new property, "disjoint simulatability"; they state the earlier QROM constructions give "less than half" the security level of their building blocks [C10].
- Jiang-Zhang-Chen-Wang-Ma (CRYPTO 2018): QROM proofs for FO^⊥̸ and FO^⊥̸_m "without suffering any ciphertext overhead" (no extra hash d), tighter than the Targhi-Unruh technique [C11].
- Bindel-Hamburg-Hövelmanns-Hülsing-Persichetti (TCC 2019) summarise the history: QROM losses went from a factor up to q^6 (TU16, HHK17) to q^2 (SXY18, JZC+18) to q (HKSU18, JZM19); for deterministic PKE their bound is tight "except for a quadratic security loss which might be impossible to avoid" [C12]. They prove: security with implicit rejection implies security with explicit rejection; the converse holds if explicit rejection also uses key confirmation; and security does not depend on whether K is derived from (m, c) or from m only [C13]. Earlier bounds carried a term q·sqrt(δ) "which is not known to be tight" [C14].
- Hövelmanns-Hülsing-Majenz (ASIACRYPT 2022, "Failing gracefully"): a reduction that handles decryption failures without the "unnatural" task of finding failures given the secret key, and that works for explicit rejection; they note that explicit-reject security "is also relevant for side channel attack resilience of the implicit-rejection variant" [C15].
- Don-Fehr-Majenz-Schaffner (EUROCRYPT 2022, per the HHM22 reference list): online extraction in the QROM, giving "the first complete post-quantum security proof of the textbook Fujisaki-Okamoto transformation" [C16].
- The Kyber team (April 2023) summarised the state for explicit rejection: the reduction "either incurs an additive q^2/sqrt(δ) term as tightness loss ... or it requires to analyze the failure bound in the extractable QROM, which has not been done so far" (quoted from the pqc-forum post as written; note the term is as the post gives it) [L11, C17].
- HHM22 also summarise the failure term of earlier QROM reductions: they bound failures through a game that hands the attacker the secret key, giving a Grover-like term 8q²·δ_wc (some papers, e.g. JZC+18, write 2q·sqrt(δ_wc)); and before their work "the only known concrete bound" for explicit rejection (DFMS) was "much weaker" than for implicit rejection. Their own explicit-rejection bound is "competitive with" the implicit-rejection state of the art [C18].
- HHM22's main QROM result (Corollary 9, PDF p. 38) is for explicit rejection FO^⊥_m. Its bound is Adv^IND-CCA ≤ Adv~ + (q_D + 1)·(2·Adv^FFP-NG + ε_δ) + ε_γ, where Adv~ = 4·sqrt((d + q_D)·Adv^IND-CPA) + 8(q + q_D)/sqrt(|M|) (d = query depth), ε_δ ≤ δ + (3 + 2δ)·sqrt(C·q_G·σ) with C = 304 and σ the variance of the failure rate over keys, and ε_γ = 24q_D(q_G + 2q_D)·2^(-γ/2) + 4q_D·2^-γ [C20]. FFP-NG is a "find a failing plaintext without the secret key" game. The authors' point: the failure loss is at most linear in q_G, where all earlier bounds had roughly q_G²·δ_wc; with a Gaussian tail bound it becomes logarithmic (Theorem 13) [C20]. The IND-CPA advantage still sits under a square root.
- **Explicit is (almost) as secure as implicit.** BHHHP19 showed implicit rejection is at least as secure as explicit. Hövelmanns and Kudinov (PQCrypto 2025, ePrint 2025/062) prove the other direction in the classical ROM: explicit rejection is as secure as implicit rejection "up to a rare edge case", which does not occur for perfectly correct schemes; they lift this to the extractable QROM, and give a looser indirect QROM argument without it [C24]. Their motivation matters for Turing: implicit rejection "can easily deteriorate into explicit rejection in practice", through timing, leaked internal flags, or "because the larger infrastructure that uses the KEM aborts connections once it notices that the key is improper" [C24]. **In a file-encryption design this is the normal case:** a wrong KEM key makes the authenticated symmetric decryption fail, and the program reports it. So a Turing KEM should be treated as explicitly rejecting at system level, and its security argument should rest on the explicit-rejection results (HHM22, HK25), not on implicit rejection hiding the failure.
- **The exact Kyber / ML-KEM variants.** Barbosa and Hülsing (ePrint 2023/755, 25 May 2023) note that Kyber's transform "is not the actual FO transform as analyzed" in the standard papers [C21]. For round-3 Kyber (K = H(K̃, H(c))) they give a direct QROM proof whose bound is 2·sqrt(Adv^OW-CPA) plus PRF, failure and injectivity terms, plus two collision terms C(q_1 + q_dec + 1)^3/|M| and C(q_2 + q_dec + 1)^3/|C_H| [C22]. Their conclusion: the round-3 parameters for levels III and V, which hash c with SHA3-256, "cannot be justified by the proven bound", and the switch to the standard FO transform "seems a good choice from a provable security point of view" [C22]. They also explain why H(ek) in G does not disturb the single-key proof: with one public key in the game, the H(pk) input can be ignored, and "hashing the public key is relevant when considering multi-instance attacks" [C21]. Maram and Xagawa (PKC 2023) gave the first direct proof of Kyber's variant [C21]. This is the history behind FIPS 203's final transform (Section 4).
- A machine-checked proof: FIPS 203 cites Almeida et al., "Formally verifying Kyber episode V" (ePrint 2024/843) for the IND-CCA2 claim [L21]. Its main theorem is in the **classical** ROM (key-derivation hash as a random oracle), down to a variant of MLWE with SHAKE-128-sampled matrices and SHAKE-256 as a PRF, for the ML-KEM *draft* specification, and it follows HHK17 "closely (but not exactly)"; it also verifies two constant-time Jasmin implementations against the specification [C19]. It is not a QROM proof. The ePrint paper was not revised after its May 2024 submission, but the project repository (formosa-crypto/formosa-mlkem, last push 2026-07-24) states that its EasyCrypt specification "is a transcription of FIPS-203" and "has, additionally, been formally proved in EasyCrypt to correctly instantiate a generic IND-CCA secure KEM construction"; the specification's key generation hashes 33 bytes, the 32-byte seed plus the byte k (`G_coins_ds`), i.e. the final standard's G(d ‖ k) [C25]. The README also says the functional-correctness proof "currently assumes that the underlying SHA-3 implementation is correct" [C25]. These are the project's own claims; this topic did not re-run the proofs.

**Plain summary for the design phase.** In the classical ROM the FO transforms are tight from IND-CPA. In the QROM every known generic bound loses at least a square root (you get roughly half the bits of the IND-CPA assumption in the worst case, for a generic attacker), and the failure rate δ enters multiplied by q or q². Nobody has found attacks that exploit this looseness for real schemes; the parameters of ML-KEM are set by the lattice attack cost, not by the QROM bound (FIPS 203 §3.2: each parameter set's security strength "was estimated based on current cryptanalysis (see Section 8 for details)", while the scheme "comes with a proof of asymptotic theoretical security in a certain heuristic model") [L22]. But the proofs only apply to the exact transform proved. A variant with a new hashing pattern, a different rejection rule, or an extra "twist" in the wrapper falls outside them.

### 3.4 Worked example: what the bounds say for ML-KEM-sized numbers

A security bound is an inequality "attacker's advantage ≤ expression". It only protects you while the expression is well below 1. The script `cca_fo_bound_examples.py` plugs ML-KEM-sized numbers into four of the published bounds [C23]:

| bound | inputs | result | what it shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| HHK17 ROM failure term q·δ | q = 2^64 hash queries; δ from FIPS 203 Table 1 | 2^-74.8 / 2^-100.8 / 2^-110.8 for ML-KEM-512 / 768 / 1024 | in the classical ROM the wrapper costs almost nothing; the lattice problem dominates |
| HHK17 QROM table bound (QFO_m) | lattice made "perfect": Adv^OW-CPA = 2^-256 (the chance of guessing m) | 2^-12.97 at q = 2^32; ≥ 1 from about q = 2^40.67 | this generic theorem says nothing beyond about 2^41 quantum queries, however hard the lattice is |
| HHM22 Cor. 9 parameter-free terms | q = d = q_D = 2^64, |M| = 2^256 | 8(q + q_D)/sqrt(|M|) = 2^-60; 4·sqrt((d + q_D)·2^-192) = 2^-61.5; (q_D + 1)·δ_768 = 2^-100.8 | the newer technique stays below 1 up to about 2^124 queries (FFP-NG and variance terms need scheme-specific work) |
| Barbosa-Hülsing collision term C(q + 1)^3/2^256 (round-3 Kyber, H(c) with SHA3-256) | C set to 1 (unknown universal constant) | 2^-64 at q = 2^64; reaches 1 near q = 2^85.3 | why round-3 levels III and V "cannot be justified by the proven bound" |

The lesson for a learner: QROM proofs for FO are qualitative evidence that the design is sound, not a source of concrete bit-security numbers. FIPS 203's security categories come from "current cryptanalysis" [L22], and FrodoKEM says the same of its QROM theorem [F11]. A custom Turing KEM would therefore need both: a proof that covers its exact transform, and a cryptanalytic estimate for its parameters (attack-cost-estimation topic).

## 4. The FO variant inside ML-KEM (FIPS 203)

(Sources read: FIPS 203 PDF pages 22-26, 41-47, 56; the pqc-forum threads that FIPS 203 cites as refs [24], [25].) Status on 2026-09-27: FIPS 203 is still the final version of August 13, 2024; the CSRC page carries a planning note dated November 17, 2025 announcing a future update/revision [L26]. The nist-pqc-standards topic read the errata sheet and reports two editorial items with no new technical requirements; nothing in it touches the transform described here (not re-checked by this topic).

The inner scheme is K-PKE (IND-CPA only). FIPS 203 §3.3 (printed p. 15) says K-PKE "shall not be used as a stand-alone cryptographic scheme" [L1].

The KEM (FIPS 203 §6, Algorithms 16-18, printed pp. 32-34):

```
KeyGen_internal(d, z):                     (Alg. 16)
  (ek_PKE, dk_PKE) = K-PKE.KeyGen(d)       inside: (rho, sigma) = G(d || k)   (Alg. 13, footnote 1)
  ek = ek_PKE
  dk = dk_PKE || ek || H(ek) || z
Encaps_internal(ek, m):                    (Alg. 17)
  (K, r) = G(m || H(ek))
  c = K-PKE.Encrypt(ek, m, r)
  return (K, c)
Decaps_internal(dk, c):                    (Alg. 18)
  parse dk = dk_PKE || ek_PKE || h || z
  m' = K-PKE.Decrypt(dk_PKE, c)
  (K', r') = G(m' || h)
  Kbar = J(z || c)                         (computed every time)
  c' = K-PKE.Encrypt(ek_PKE, m', r')
  if c != c' then K' = Kbar                (implicit rejection)
  return K'
```
with G = SHA3-512 split into two 32-byte halves, H = SHA3-256, J = SHAKE256 with 32-byte output (FIPS 203 §4.1, eqs. 4.4-4.5) [L23].

What each piece is for, according to the designers (pqc-forum, "Kyber decisions, part 2: FO transform", Kyber team, Dec 2022 = FIPS 203 ref [25]):
- H(ek) inside G makes the key depend on both parties ("contributory") and, hashed into the coins r, "gives protection against multi-target attacks exploiting decryption failures" [L7].
- Round-3 Kyber also hashed H(c) into the final key; the same post says this "does not help at all with any formal security property or with proofs" and complicates QROM proofs [L8].

Differences from round-3 Kyber (FIPS 203 Appendix C.1, printed p. 47) [L3-L6]:
1. Shared secret fixed at 256 bits.
2. Different FO variant: Encaps "no longer includes a hash of the ciphertext in the derivation of the shared secret".
3. Round 3 hashed the randomness first (m <- H(m)) "to safeguard against the use of flawed randomness generation processes"; FIPS 203 drops it because the standard "requires the use of NIST-approved randomness generation".
4. Explicit input checks were added (Section 7).

Changes from the draft FIPS 203 ipd (Appendix C.2): domain separation G(d || k) was added "to prevent the misuse of keys generated to target one security level from being used for a different security level when saving a key as a seed", and the swapped matrix indices of the ipd were reverted to match Kyber [L9, L10].

The Kyber team's own summary of the final transform (pqc-forum, 7 Apr 2023, FIPS 203 ref [24]): "this is the standard FO transform with implicit rejection, except that the hash of the public key is fed as an additional argument into G". The reasons given: about 17% faster AVX2 encapsulation and simpler proofs with better bounds; the one disadvantage named is the temptation to compute the rejection key only on failure, which "creates a timing side-channel that essentially gives an attacker explicit rejection" [L11, L12]. FIPS 203 answers that by requiring the reject flag to be destroyed and never returned in any form (§6.3, printed p. 33) [L13].

In HHK17's vocabulary, ML-KEM is T followed by U^⊥̸_m: K is derived from m (and H(ek)) only, not from c, and the rejection key is F(z, c) with F = J. Starfighters describe it exactly this way, with H = the first 256 bits of G (SHA3-512) and F = J (SHAKE256) [L25]. BHHHP19's result that deriving K from m or from (m, c) makes no difference to IND-CCA [C13] is what makes dropping H(c) safe.

## 5. The FO variant inside FrodoKEM

**Round 3 (2021) = today's eFrodoKEM.** The round-3 specification (§2.2.8, PDF pp. 19-20) applies HHK17's FO^⊥̸ with three changes, calling the result FO^⊥̸': one hash with longer output gives both r and k; the computation of r and k takes the public key (its hash pkh = G1(pk)) as input; the shared secret ss = F(c ‖ k) also takes the ciphertext as input [F1]:

```
Encaps(pk):  µ ← random;  (r, k) = G2(G1(pk) ‖ µ);  c = Enc(µ, pk; r);  ss = F(c ‖ k)
Decaps(c):   µ' = Dec(c, sk); (r', k') = G2(pkh ‖ µ'); ss0 = F(c ‖ k'); ss1 = F(c ‖ s)
             (in constant time) ss = ss0 if c = Enc(µ', pk; r') else ss1
```
The spec warns that "a timing side-channel enables key recovery if step 5 of KEM^⊥̸'.Decaps is not performed in constant time", citing Guo, Johansson and Nilsson [F2].

**The salted version (ISO proposal; preliminary standardization proposal dated 2023-03-14, revised 2024-12-05 and 2025-09-29).** The document now defines two families [F3]:
- **FrodoKEM** (salted), for applications where "a large number of ciphertexts may be encrypted to a single public key";
- **eFrodoKEM** (ephemeral), identical to round-3 FrodoKEM, only for applications that produce "a small number of ciphertexts (e.g., 2^8)" per public key [F4].

Salted encapsulation (§8.2) [F5]:
```
u, salt ← random (len_sec and len_salt bits)
pkh = SHAKE(pk, len_sec)
seedSE ‖ k = SHAKE(pkh ‖ u ‖ salt, len_SE + len_sec)
(c1, c2) = FrodoPKE encryption of u with coins from seedSE
ss = SHAKE(c1 ‖ c2 ‖ salt ‖ k, len_sec);  ciphertext c = c1 ‖ c2 ‖ salt
```
Decapsulation (§8.3) recomputes seedSE' ‖ k' from (pkh ‖ u' ‖ salt), re-encrypts, and "(In constant time)" picks k̄ = k' if B'‖C = B''‖C' else k̄ = s; then ss = SHAKE(c1 ‖ c2 ‖ salt ‖ k̄) [F6]. So FrodoKEM keeps implicit rejection, keeps the ciphertext in the final hash, and adds the salt to both the coins and the key.

Sizes (Table A.2): salted FrodoKEM-640/976/1344 use len_SE = len_salt = 256 / 384 / 512 bits; eFrodoKEM uses len_SE = 128 / 192 / 256 and no salt [F7]. For all sets len_A = 128 bits and len_sec = 128 / 192 / 256 (Table A.1) [F8]. The script `cca_multitarget_bounds.py` checks len_salt = len_SE = 2·len_sec [F7].

Why the change: see Section 8. Other design points the proposal states explicitly:
- A fresh matrix A per key (seed_A = SHAKE(z)) "to eliminate the possibility of backdoors and to reduce the risk of all-for-the-price-of-one attacks" (§9.4) [F9].
- pkh in the coins "to reduce the risk of batch attacks targeting multiple keys", while the round-3 analysis "does not formally cover security in the multi-target setting" (§9.5.1) [F10].
- Reductions (§9.2): tight classical-ROM IND-CCA from IND-CPA of FrodoPKE with rounded Gaussian errors (Theorem 5.1 of the spec); non-tight QROM IND-CCA from OW-CPA (Theorem 5.8), which "does not concretely support the bit-security" of the parameter sets [F11].
- Failure boosting (§9.3): based on D'Anvers et al. 2019 the parameters "suffer no loss in their claimed security"; FrodoKEM-1344's intrinsic failure probability is 2^-252.5 (decryption-failures topic owns this) [F12].

## 6. Binding properties of KEMs

**Why IND-CCA is not enough.** IND-CCA says the key looks random to someone without the secret key. It says nothing about whether the key is tied to one public key or one ciphertext. Cremers, Dax and Medinger (CCS 2024; full version 1.1.3, 25 Nov 2024, ePrint 2023/1933) found with the Tamarin prover that an authenticated key exchange from the original Kyber paper is secure with Kyber but not with every IND-CCA KEM: a "re-encapsulation attack" decapsulates to a key k and then produces a second ciphertext, for a different public key, that decapsulates to the same k. At protocol level this is typically an unknown-key-share attack [B1].

**The notions (CDM §4).** X-BIND-P-Q means "P binds Q": an attacker cannot produce two runs where the values in P agree but some value in Q differs. P and Q range over the shared key K, ciphertext CT and public key PK. X is the attacker's power [B2]:
- HON: honestly generated keys, attacker has decapsulation oracles;
- LEAK: honestly generated keys, attacker also gets both secret keys;
- MAL: attacker builds the key pairs itself (possibly malformed).
MAL implies LEAK implies HON [B3]. Example: MAL-BIND-K-PK = "even with keys it built itself, the attacker cannot get the same shared key under two different public keys".

**Implicit rejection limits what is possible.** An implicitly rejecting KEM never outputs ⊥, so a ciphertext alone can never bind anything: no implicitly rejecting KEM satisfies X-BIND-CT-PK or X-BIND-CT-K (CDM Theorem 4.10) [B4]. Its rejection key must be computed from a secret random value and the rejected ciphertext (CDM Lemma 4.9, stated informally) [B5]. That leaves 12 key-binding notions (four general notions × three attacker levels) [B6].

**ML-KEM's binding status (as of the sources read).**
- LEAK-BIND-K-CT and LEAK-BIND-K-PK hold (Fiedler-Günther, as cited in CDM Table 5) [B7].
- Schmieg (Google, 3 Apr 2024, ePrint 2024/523): ML-KEM is neither MAL-BIND-K-CT nor MAL-BIND-K-PK [B8]. Both counterexamples need malformed decapsulation keys:
  - K-CT: the attacker puts another key's hash h0 into the stored field h of dk1. Decapsulation then derives (K, r) = G(m ‖ h0) and the key matches a different ciphertext under a different public key [B9].
  - K-PK: two otherwise honest keys share the same rejection seed z; a random ciphertext is rejected by both and both output J(z ‖ c) [B10]. These keys "appear well-formed" to an auditor [B10].
- Mitigations (Schmieg §3): omit h from the stored key and recompute H(ek) (proves MAL-BIND-K-CT); or check H(ek) = h when loading the key (this is FIPS 203's §7.3 hash check [L18]); or store keys as a single seed from which (σ, ρ, z) are derived. Only the single-seed option gives MAL-BIND-K-PK, "as long as z is not drawn independently of the rest of the seed" [B11].
- FIPS 203 final allows storing the seed (d, z) [L16], but d and z are two independent 32-byte values. So storing (d, z) does not give MAL-BIND-K-PK: an attacker who supplies both halves can reuse z. CDM (§7.2) say this directly for the draft: "implicitly rejecting KEMs with such independent values cannot achieve our MAL-BIND-K-PK and MAL-BIND-K,CT-PK properties just by performing simple sanity checks" [B12]. The earlier-run property test 5b reproduces this: two keys with a shared z give the same K for a random ciphertext, even when regenerated from their seeds [B13].
- Krämer-Struck-Weishäupl ("Binding security of implicitly-rejecting KEMs and application to BIKE and HQC") complete the table. For ML-KEM (their Table 2): MAL-BIND-K-PK ✗, MAL-BIND-K,CT-PK ✗, MAL-BIND-K-CT ✗, MAL-BIND-K,PK-CT ✓; all LEAK and HON key-binding notions ✓ [B14].

**How to get all binding notions (KSW Table 3, Theorems 14-15)** [B15]:

| transform | key when c is valid | key when c is rejected | HON | LEAK | MAL |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FO_H (= HHK FO^⊥̸) | H(m, c) | H(σ, c) | ✓* | ✗ | ✗ |
| FO_L (= ML-KEM's) | H(m, hpk) | H(σ, c) | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| FO_M (new) | H(m, hpk) | H(σ, hpk, c) | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

(* = relies on a mild assumption.) FO_M differs from ML-KEM only by hashing hpk into the rejection key too. KSW argue IND-CCA is preserved "since we just add a public value to the key derivation" [B16]. For full MAL binding they also require the stored hpk to be checked against the public key once after key generation (§5.1.3) [B17]. The earlier-run property test 5b shows the FO_M rejection key J(z ‖ H(ek) ‖ c) separates the two shared-z keys [B13].

**Binding with explicit rejection.** Because a file format makes rejection explicit (Section 3.3), the explicit-rejection picture matters too. Krämer, Münz, Struck and Weishäupl (ePrint 2025/2129, document dated 2025-11-21; IACR CiC 2(4), 2026 per the Starfighters reference list) analyse FO^⊥, FO^⊥_m and the variants with a plaintext-confirmation hash (HFO^⊥, HFO^⊥_m) [B31]. Explicit rejection allows the ciphertext-binds notions that implicit rejection can never reach. Their results are "mostly positive" for the explicit FO transform, "though many notions require further robustness assumptions on the underlying PKE"; HFO "achieves all notions and requires weaker robustness assumptions"; and a slightly modified HFO "achieves all binding notions without requiring any robustness of the underlying PKE" [B31]. The detailed theorems were not transcribed here.

**A salt that is left out of the key hash breaks binding: the HQC case.** KSW also analysed round-4 HQC, whose salted FO variant appends a random salt to the ciphertext and feeds it into the coins, but does not hash it into the shared key or the rejection key (k = H_U(m, (u, v)) or H_U(σ, (u, v))) [B25]. Result: HQC achieves only 2 of the 12 binding notions (HON-BIND-K-PK and HON-BIND-K,CT-PK) [B25]. The HON-BIND-K-CT attack needs no secret at all: two invalid ciphertexts that differ only in the salt are both rejected with the same key H_U(σ, (u, v)) (Theorem 12) [B26]. With the leaked σ it gets worse: encapsulate with m = σ, then change the salt; the valid and the rejected ciphertext give the same key (Theorem 10, LEAK-BIND-K,PK-CT) [B26]. KSW's fix, HQC*, hashes the entire ciphertext, salt included, into both keys. It then gets exactly the FO^⊥̸ results, and KSW state that the salted way of deriving coins "is irrelevant for our FO̸⊥ results" (Theorem 13) [B27]. FrodoKEM's salted variant already hashes c1 ‖ c2 ‖ salt into the final key on both paths [F5, F6]. Rule for Turing: every byte of the ciphertext, salt included, must enter both the accept-path and the reject-path key derivation. The property test 6 reproduces both attacks on a toy salted FO built from ML-KEM's K-PKE: with the salt left out, two different ciphertexts give the same key, and with a leaked rejection secret a valid and a rejected ciphertext collide; with the whole ciphertext hashed, neither happens [B28].

CDM's generic alternative (§4.8, §7.2): post-process the KEM output with a KDF over (K, CT, PK); ML-KEM cannot put c into its own key derivation because K and the coins r come from the same G call, before c exists [B18].

**What a combiner inherits (brief; hybrid-combiners topic owns the details).** Krämer-Struck-Weishäupl ("Binding security of combined KEMs") show that a generic combiner achieves all 12 "key binds X" notions if Combine is collision-resistant and takes both public keys and both ciphertexts as input. If inputs are dropped, the combiner keeps a notion only if the component KEM has it. The ciphertext-binds notions behave differently: X-BIND-CT-K needs both KEMs to have it [B19]. Connolly et al. analyse X-Wing-style combiners concurrently [B19].

**Contributory keys.** The Kyber team's reason for H(pk) in G was to make the key depend on both parties; CDM formalise "contributory" as LEAK-BIND-K-PK [B20].

**Ciphertext second-preimage resistance (C2PRI): the property X-Wing needs from its lattice KEM.** X-Wing's combiner (QSF) does not hash the lattice ciphertext or lattice public key into the final key. That is only safe if the lattice KEM is C2PRI: given an honest encapsulation (K*, c*), nobody can find a different c ≠ c* that decapsulates to the same K* (Starfighters, ePrint 2025/1397, to appear at IEEE S&P 2026) [B21]. Starfighters prove C2PRI for every U-transform variant applied to a rigid deterministic PKE, which covers the full FO transform because T produces a rigid PKE [B22]. For U^⊥̸_m with independent H and F (the ML-KEM case, H = the key half of G and F = J) the classical bound is (q_H + q_F + 3)/|K|. For the salted FO the bounds are the same as the unsalted ones (Proposition 28) [B23]. Their Table 3 gives ML-KEM (q + 3)/2^256 classically and 8(2q + 3)^2/2^256 quantumly; for (e)FrodoKEM-640 it is (q + 2)/2^128, because FrodoKEM-640's key hash is only 128 bits [B24]. Two consequences for Turing: (1) a custom KEM that is meant to drop into an X-Wing-style combiner must be an FO variant covered by these theorems, with a 256-bit key hash; (2) a combiner that hashes the full lattice ciphertext and public key does not need C2PRI (hybrid-combiners topic owns the combiner side). Starfighters note that H(m) and F must not reuse one hash without domain separation, "as to not leak the shared key" (their footnote 19, citing Bellare-Davis-Günther) [B22].

**Robustness and anonymity (for multi-recipient or recipient-hiding files).** Grubbs, Maram and Paterson (EUROCRYPT 2022, ePrint 2021/708) study two properties beyond IND-CCA. Anonymity: a ciphertext does not reveal which public key it was made for. Robustness: a receiver doing trial decryption is "not fooled into accepting a plaintext intended for someone else" [B29]. Their findings [B29, B30]: an implicit-rejection KEM "cannot be robust" (it never outputs ⊥), and such KEMs do not in general pass anonymity and robustness on to the KEM-DEM encryption built from them; but the FO^⊥̸ transform does enhance anonymity and collision-freeness, so FO-built KEM-DEM schemes can get around the negative result. Concretely they give "positive robustness results for Saber, Kyber, and FrodoKEM", and show that with Classic McEliece "for any message m, we can construct a single hybrid ciphertext c which decrypts to the chosen m under any Classic McEliece private key". For anonymity they found barriers for Kyber that also affected its round-3 IND-CCA claims; this is the ePrint 2021/708 work the Kyber team cited when dropping H(c) [L8]. Maram and Xagawa (PKC 2023, "Post-quantum anonymity of Kyber") continued this; that paper was not opened here. For Turing: if one file can be encrypted to several recipients, or the file should not reveal its recipient, robustness and anonymity become requirements, and they must be argued for the whole KEM-DEM file format, not just for the KEM.

## 7. Randomness: weak RNGs, hedging, and seeds

**Where randomness enters an FO KEM.** In ML-KEM exactly three random 32-byte values are drawn: d and z in KeyGen (Alg. 19) and m in Encaps (Alg. 20). Everything else is derived deterministically: the internal functions "are all deterministic, meaning that their output is completely determined by their input" (FIPS 203 §6) [R1]. So:
- **Weak m ⇒ weak shared key.** K and the coins r are G(m ‖ H(ek)) (Alg. 17). H(ek) is public, so the shared key has no more unpredictability than m. Anyone who can guess m recomputes K and c without touching the lattice. (This is read off Alg. 17; the earlier-run property test 3 confirms Encaps_internal is a deterministic function of (ek, m) [R2].) FrodoKEM has the same structure (seedSE ‖ k from pkh ‖ u ‖ salt; the salt is public) [F5].
- **Weak d ⇒ weak key pair.** (ρ, σ) = G(d ‖ k), so the whole key pair is a function of d; with t bits of entropy in d an attacker enumerates 2^t key pairs. The real-world precedent is RSA/DSA: Heninger et al. (USENIX Security 2012) found 0.75% of TLS certificates sharing keys "due to insufficient entropy during key generation" and factored RSA keys of 0.50% of TLS hosts, mostly headless or embedded devices, with "a boot-time entropy hole in the Linux random number generator" [R3].
- **Weak or leaked z.** z only protects the rejection path. If z is known, the rejection key J(z ‖ c) is computable, so implicit rejection becomes explicit rejection (the attacker learns whether a ciphertext was rejected). IND-CCA then rests on the explicit-rejection proofs (BHHHP19 Theorem 3: implicit-reject security implies explicit-reject security only in that direction; HHM22 give direct explicit-reject bounds) [C13, C15]. This is a derived observation, not a claim from a paper.
- **Worked demonstration** (`cca_weak_randomness_demo.py`, ML-KEM-512, toy RNG with a 12-bit state for Encaps and 8-bit for KeyGen) [R17]. (1) From (ek, c) alone, enumerating the RNG states recomputes Encaps_internal until the ciphertext matches, recovering K after 3,002 Encaps calls (20 s in pure Python). (2) Hashing the RNG output first, as round-3 Kyber did, changes nothing: the key is found after the same 3,002 guesses. (3) Hedging, m = SHAKE256(secret ‖ RNG output ‖ ek), leaves the attacker 0 matches in 8,192 guesses, and the receiver decapsulates it with unchanged ML-KEM code. (4) With an 8-bit key-generation seed the attacker rebuilds dk from ek and decapsulates an honest ciphertext. A real RNG failure is the same attack with a larger count.
- **Repeated m.** The Kyber round-3 spec: "What is not safe, is to reuse the same randomness in encapsulation" [R4].

**FIPS 203's rules.** Fresh randomness for every KeyGen and Encaps call from an approved RBG (SP 800-90A/B/C) with security strength at least 128 / 192 / 256 bits for ML-KEM-512 / 768 / 1024 [L15]; the random draws "shall be performed by the cryptographic module" and the derandomised internal interfaces "should not be made available to applications other than for testing purposes" (§3.3) [R5]; KeyGen and Encaps return an error if the RBG fails (Alg. 19 lines 3-4, Alg. 20 lines 2-3) [R6].

**Why FIPS 203 dropped m ← H(m).** Round-3 Kyber hashed the RNG output first, with the comment "Do not send output of system RNG" (round-3 spec, Alg. 8 line 2) [R7]. FIPS 203 dropped it because the standard requires approved randomness [L5]. The pqc-forum debate (FIPS 203 ref [24]): Schwabe (Kyber team) argued the hash is "a cheap way to not leak the output of whatever RNG is used on the system" outside FIPS settings; Saarinen argued it is redundant under SP 800-90 RBGs and, in masked implementations, "unnecessarily correlates the otherwise independent, uniform shares"; NIST (31 May 2023) decided not to include it, noting that an attacker who backdoors the RBG "to learn all future outputs" is not much hindered by an extra hash [L14, R8]. The point everyone agreed on: hashing does not add entropy. It only hides raw RNG output from an observer, which matters for a generator whose outputs reveal its future outputs (a forum participant cites Dual_EC_DRBG as that case).

**Hedging.** Bellare et al. (ASIACRYPT 2009; ePrint 2012/220) define hedged PKE: IND-CPA with good randomness, and a weaker but still useful notion (IND-CDA) when randomness is bad instead of "breaking completely"; they give "simple RO-based ways to make in-practice IND-CPA schemes hedge secure" [R9]. The standard deployment form is RFC 8937 (IRTF/CFRG, Informational, October 2020): replace the RNG G(n) by G'(n) = Expand(Extract(H(Sig(sk, tag1)), G(L)), tag2, n), mixing a secret long-term value into every draw; Sig must be deterministic and Sig(sk, tag1) "MUST NOT be used or exposed beyond its role in this computation" [R10]. Boldyreva, Patton and Shrimpton (CRYPTO 2017) describe the standard hedging construction as "hashing all of the encryption inputs" and lift the best-possible hedged notion (IND-CDA) to the CCA setting [R16]. For a KEM the analogous step would be m = PRF(secret state, RNG output, ek, context) before Encaps. Note the FIPS 203 constraint: an FO KEM's Encaps must still look like Encaps_internal(ek, m) with m uniform; hedging changes how m is produced, not the KEM, so it stays interoperable. (Design idea; no paper analyses hedging for ML-KEM specifically: Open questions.)

**Seeds and deterministic key generation.**
- FIPS 203 allows storing (d, z) instead of dk; the seed "shall be treated with the same safeguards as a decapsulation key" [L16]. Domain separation G(d ‖ k) makes a seed expanded under the wrong parameter set give an unrelated key [L9]; the earlier-run property test 2 checks one d gives three different ρ [R11].
- SP 800-227 (final, September 2025): a key-generation seed may be stored as private data "for the purpose of recomputing the same key pair at a later time"; stored private data must be secure against leakage and unauthorised modification; formats (seed or expanded) are an implementation choice [R12].
- RFC 9935 (IETF Proposed Standard, March 2026) for ML-KEM in X.509: private key as a 64-byte seed d ‖ z, the expanded key, or both; "the seed-only format is RECOMMENDED"; importers of "both" SHOULD run a seed consistency check and MUST reject inconsistent keys [R13]. Its §9 states that the 64-byte seed format "additionally provides MAL-BIND-K-CT security (but still does not provide security for MAL-BIND-K-PK)" [R14], matching Section 6 above.
- Single-seed keys (d and z derived from one seed) are what Schmieg's Proposition 2 needs for MAL-BIND-K-PK [B11]. X-Wing derives its ML-KEM key from one 32-byte seed (hybrid-combiners topic; not re-verified here).

## 8. Multi-target and multi-user security

**Vocabulary.** Multi-key (multi-user) attacks try to break any one of many public keys. Multi-ciphertext attacks target one key but try to break any one of many ciphertexts. "Multi-target" covers both; the FrodoKEM authors avoid the term because it is used for both meanings [M1].

**Generic fact.** Single-user IND-CCA implies multi-user security with a loss linear in the number of users times challenges (Bellare-Boldyreva-Micali 2000, as cited by Duman et al. and by GHS) [M2]. A linear loss of 2^32 users × 2^64 ciphertexts is 96 bits, so the generic argument alone can leave very little.

**Three defences with published analysis.**

1. **Per-key public matrix (per-key seed).** Both ML-KEM (ρ from G(d ‖ k), per key) and FrodoKEM (seed_A per key) derive the lattice matrix from a per-key seed. FrodoKEM's stated reason: a fixed A could be backdoored and would make "all the secure connections rely on a single instance of a lattice problem"; per-key A reduces "the risk of all-for-the-price-of-one attacks" [F9]. The origin is NewHope (Alkim, Ducas, Pöppelmann, Schwabe, USENIX Security 2016, §3). It gives two concrete reasons [M15]. (a) Backdoor: an authority could choose a = g·f^-1 with small f, g (an NTRU-style trapdoor) and then recover secrets from any (a, b = a·s + e). (b) Precomputation: an attacker could spend massive work once on the fixed a (e.g. a reduced basis for its lattice) and then decode every exchange "using for example Babai's decoding algorithm", which the authors compare to the "Logjam" attack on fixed Diffie-Hellman primes. Their remedy, "Against all authority", is a fresh a per instance. The same section warns that short-term caching of ephemeral lattice key-exchange secrets, as TLS libraries do for Diffie-Hellman, "would be disastrous for security" [M15], which is Section 2's key-reuse problem. Kyber adopted this "against-all-authority" approach from NewHope: re-generating A per public key "protects against an attacker attempting to break many keys at the cost of breaking one key" (Kyber round-3 spec §4.5.3) [M11]; see lattice-foundations / attack-cost-estimation topics for the lattice side.
2. **Hash (part of) the public key into the coins and key.** Duman, Hövelmanns, Kiltz, Lyubashevsky, Seiler (CCS 2021; ePrint 2021/1351 per the Kyber team's post [L7]) prove that including the public key "is indeed important for the tightness of the security reductions in the ROM and the QROM", and that "it is enough to include just a small (e.g. 32 byte) unpredictable part of the key" [M3]. Their Figure 1 bounds (ROM): with FO^⊥̸_m (no pk) the correctness term is n·q_F·δ(1); with pk or ID(pk) hashed it is q_F·δ(n), where δ(n) is the n-user correctness error and δ(1) ≤ δ(n) ≤ n·δ(1); in the QROM the no-pk variant carries a factor n·q_C in front [M4]. ML-KEM hashes all of ek (H(ek)); FrodoKEM hashes pkh = SHAKE(pk).
3. **A public random salt per encapsulation (salted FO, SFO).** Because FO makes encryption deterministic in the message m, an attacker holding n_c ciphertexts for one key can encrypt N messages of its own and look for a collision; the probability is about n_c·N/|M|. For FrodoKEM-640 (|M| = 2^128) and n_c = 2^40 this needs about N = 2^88 encryptions; NIST found this privately in 2021 and Bernstein published it (ePrint 2022/1580); the 128-bit seed_SE had the same problem [M5]. The fix was a public salt in the coins and in the key, and a longer seed_SE [M5, F5, F7]. Glabush-Hövelmanns-Stebila (IACR CiC vol. 3 no. 1, accepted 2026-04-22; ePrint 2025/343) proved the salted transform: in the classical ROM, multi-target IND-CCA of the KEM "tightly reduces to the multi-target IND-CPA security of the underlying PKE", recovering "62 bits of multi-target security" for 128-bit-message schemes such as FrodoKEM-640 and HQC-128 [M6]. Their example (|M| = 2^128, len_salt = 256, n_c = 2^64, n_u = 2^32): the salted collision term is 2^-224, while the unsalted generic bound is vacuous [M7].

**Does this matter for a 256-bit-message scheme like ML-KEM?** GHS §6.2 give two caveats [M8]:
- ML-KEM uses 256-bit messages at all three levels. At a 256-bit target, "an adversary generating 2^128 ciphertexts will find a collision with one of 2^64 challenge ciphertexts with probability about 2^-64", so "without salting, even ML-KEM-1024 can only achieve 192 bits of multi-target security in the random oracle model".
- In the QROM the collision terms scale as a square root: with q_RO = 2^40 the term is 2^-74 without salt and 2^-105 with salt (t ≈ 4).
All numbers in this subsection are recomputed by `cca_multitarget_bounds.py` [M9]. GHS also say plainly that they "do not bound the multi-target IND-CPA security" of the underlying PKEs of HQC, FrodoKEM or ML-KEM [M10]: salting fixes the generic collision term only.

**A dissent on the lattice part: Bernstein, ePrint 2022/1580 (version dated 2023-03-17).** The salt fixes the generic FO collision term; it does nothing about attacks on the lattice problem itself across many ciphertexts. Bernstein argues, under the standard heuristics for primal lattice attacks and asymptotically, that "typical lattice PKEs asymptotically degrade in heuristic multi-ciphertext IND-CPA security as the number of ciphertexts increases", that this implies multi-ciphertext IND-CCA attacks on typical lattice KEMs, and that the degradation is exponential in Θ(n / log n) for breaking one out of many ciphertexts [M12]. He also claims "flaws in the claim of an MLWE-based proof of tight multi-target security" in Duman et al. (CCS 2021), and gives the 2^88-guess attack on one of 2^40 FrodoKEM-640 ciphertexts [M12]. His own caveats: the analysis is heuristic and asymptotic, Kyber-512 may be "too small for the heuristic multi-target speedups to be applicable", and he concludes that the standard heuristics and "multi-target equals single-target" cannot both be right [M13]. GHS side with Bernstein on the specific point: their appendix calls Duman et al.'s page-3 claim that Adv^IND-CPA ≈ Adv^IND(nc,nu)-CPA for Kyber's PKE "a false claim", "refuted by Bernstein [Ber22], as well as by Peikert in private communication", because each encryption uses its own MLWE secret [M14]. GHS also found a bug in the proofs of Duman et al.'s Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 (a flag that misses one order of events) and fixed it in their own Theorem 3 [M14]. So the multi-user bounds cited in point 2 above [M3, M4] should be read in GHS's corrected form. The multi-ciphertext security of the lattice problem itself is an open research question, not a settled fact in either direction; GHS explicitly leave it unbounded [M10]. For Turing this argues for two cheap habits: limit the number of ciphertexts per long-term key (key rotation), and do not spend the parameter margin on a tight single-target estimate (attack-cost-estimation topic).

**Key reuse.** FO-transformed KEMs are designed for key reuse (FrodoKEM "does not impose any restriction on the reuse of key pairs" [F3]); the IND-CPA inner schemes are not (Section 2). The cost of reuse is the multi-ciphertext loss above, which a salt addresses.

## 9. Input validation and malformed inputs

**What FIPS 203 requires** (Section 7) [L17, L18]:

| input | check | when |
|---|---|---|
| ek | length 384k + 32; every 12-bit coefficient < q, tested as ByteEncode12(ByteDecode12(ek[0:384k])) = ek[0:384k] | before Encaps; may be assured once or "through other means" |
| c | length 32(d_u·k + d_v) | every Decaps |
| dk | length 768k + 96; H(dk[384k : 768k+32]) = dk[768k+32 : 768k+64] | before Decaps; may be assured once |
| (ek, dk) pair, optional | seed consistency, ek check, dk check, pair-wise Encaps/Decaps | on import from a third party |

FIPS 203 is explicit that these checks do not prove a key was honestly generated [L17, L19]. SP 800-227 §3.2 allows skipping re-checks if the module generated or checked the input and stored it "in a manner that prevents modification" [R15].

**Why each check matters.**
- The modulus check keeps the encapsulation key a valid element of the ring. Without it a coefficient ≥ q is silently reduced, so two different byte strings would be the same key (a malleability and binding problem) and implementations could disagree.
- The dk hash check is exactly the fix for Schmieg's MAL-BIND-K-CT counterexample [B9, B11].
- The ciphertext check keeps parsing well-defined. Beyond length, ML-KEM ciphertexts have no invalid encodings: ByteDecode_d for d < 12 reduces modulo 2^d, so every byte string of the right length decodes and decompresses [L24]. Everything else is left to the FO re-encryption comparison.
- The comparison c = c' must be over all bytes and constant time. A strcmp()-style comparison stops at a zero byte and wrongly accepts some ciphertexts; CCTV gives the random chance as 2^-16 [V1].

**A skipped FO check is invisible to ordinary tests: the HQC precedent and a published fix.** Glabush, Günther, Hövelmanns and Stebila (CRYPTO 2025, ePrint 2025/450) report that HQC's reference implementation had a flawed re-encryption check that accepted malformed ciphertexts; it was copied downstream (liboqs) and "only noticed after 19 months", because honest ciphertexts still decrypted correctly [V6]. Their fix is an FO variant with **confirmation codes** (FO_C = U_C ∘ T_C): encryption also outputs a short code that only a full re-encryption can recompute, and the code goes into the key derivation. A decapsulator that skips re-encryption then produces wrong keys, so a plain encaps/decaps round-trip test fails [V6]. For ML-KEM (their ML-KEM_C, Fig. 14) the code is a few uncompressed ciphertext coefficients, the single SHA3-512 call becomes two SHA3-256 calls, and K = SHA3-256(m ‖ H(ek) ‖ cd); ciphertexts and keys keep ML-KEM's format, but K differs from ML-KEM's [V7]. With 12-20-byte codes one correctness test catches a faulty implementation with probability about 1/3 or more, at most 3.4% runtime overhead; their Table 1 gives guessing probabilities 0.1712 (512, 768) and 0.4114 (1024) for |S| = 2 under a uniformity assumption [V7]. They prove FO_C keeps IND-CCA in the ROM and QROM [V6]. This is a published, proven "twist" aimed at exactly Turing's second goal (verified releases); its cost is loss of ML-KEM byte compatibility for K. It is also being considered for a standard: NIST's FIPS 207 (HQC-KEM) slides (PDF dated 2025-09-24) list a "1 byte confirmation code" from Glabush et al. among the "Changes to HQC Round 4 spec under consideration", to "Protect against faulty implementations that skip or incorrectly implement the re-encryption step", and request feedback on it [V9]. The same slides list the switch to a salted transform with (ek_KEM, salt) in the derivation of K and θ, which "fixes the IND-CCA2 issue that was raised on the pqc-forum" and "improves HQC security properties beyond IND-CCA" [V9], i.e. the salt now enters the key hash, as Section 6 requires.

Lesson for Turing: every rejection rule needs a test vector that fails when the rule is removed, and the test suite should be run once against a deliberately broken build (a negative control) to prove it notices. Known-answer tests on honest inputs alone would pass all four bugs above, and ordinary round-trip tests let HQC's broken check through for 19 months [V6]. Every ciphertext-dependent key-derivation input (including any salt) also needs a "changed only this field" vector, which is how the HQC salt-binding flaw shows up [B26, B28].

## 10. Checklist for a custom lattice KEM

A lattice KEM for Turing is a robust IND-CCA KEM only if every applicable item holds (items 10, 20, 22 and 23 apply only in the situations they name; item 22 is optional). "Source" is where the requirement or the evidence comes from; a later design phase should turn each item into a test.

| # | requirement | why / evidence | source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The inner PKE is IND-CPA under a named lattice assumption, with a concrete attack-cost estimate for the exact parameters, and is never exposed or used alone | IND-CPA schemes with reused keys fall to a few thousand match/mismatch queries | FIPS 203 §3.3 [L1]; Fluhrer [K1-K4]; Qin et al. [K8]; estimate: attack-cost-estimation topic |
| 2 | Use a published FO variant exactly: derandomise with coins = hash(m, …), re-encrypt on decapsulation, compare, and derive K by hashing | T + U is what turns OW/IND-CPA into IND-CCA; proofs cover only the exact transform | HHK17 [C2-C4]; FIPS 203 Alg. 17-18 [L2] |
| 3 | Message space #M ≥ 2^κ for κ-bit security (256-bit m for a 256-bit target) | the q_RO/#M term in the FO bound | HHK17 §3.3 [C5] |
| 4 | Correctness error δ ≤ 2^-κ, computed exactly for the parameters, and failure-boosting analysed | q_RO·δ term; failures leak the key | HHK17 [C5]; FrodoKEM §9.3 [F12]; decryption-failures topic |
| 5 | Implicit rejection with K̄ = H(z, hpk, c): secret z, full ciphertext, hash of the public key | rejection key needs secret + ciphertext; adding hpk gives MAL binding (FO_M) | CDM Lemma 4.9 [B5]; KSW Thm. 15 [B15] |
| 6 | Compute the rejection key on every call; select K or K̄ in constant time; never output or log the reject flag. | 7 | Compare the full ciphertext with a constant-time equality, never strcmp/memcmp-with-early-exit | strcmp accepts some invalid ciphertexts | CCTV/Wycheproof strcmp [V1, V3] |

| 10 | If one key receives many ciphertexts and 256-bit multi-target security is claimed: add a public random salt (≥ 2κ bits) to the coin hash, hash the whole ciphertext including the salt into both the accept-path and reject-path keys, and use a coin seed of ≥ 2κ bits | unsalted FO: ML-KEM-1024 reaches only 192-bit multi-target security in the ROM; salted FO has a tight proof; a salt left out of the key hash broke HQC's binding even for honest keys | GHS [M6-M8]; FrodoKEM Table A.2, §8.2-8.3 [F5-F7]; KSW Thm 10, 12, 13 [B25-B27] |

| 21 | Limit the number of ciphertexts per long-term key (rotation), and say what the limit is | multi-ciphertext security of the lattice problem is not bounded by any proof and is disputed; unsalted 128-bit-message schemes are limited to about 2^8 ciphertexts per key by their own designers | Bernstein [M12, M13]; GHS [M10, M14]; eFrodoKEM [F4] |

## What this means for Turing

The owner wants a lattice layer "with a twist" that is harder to break. This topic covers only the wrapper that turns lattice encryption into a KEM. Here the literature is clear about which twists have published support and which add risk. docs/02 planned X-Wing (X25519 + ML-KEM-768) from vetted implementations; nothing below argues against that plan.

**Options with published analysis (they help, at small cost):**

1. **Salted FO (the strongest evidence-backed "twist").** Turing targets 256-bit keys (docs/02). GHS §6.2: without a salt, even ML-KEM-1024 "can only achieve 192 bits of multi-target security in the random oracle model", and in the QROM the collision term for q = 2^40 improves from 2^-74 to 2^-105 with a salt [M8]. FrodoKEM already ships the salted variant with a 2κ-bit salt [F5, F7], and GHS prove it tight in the classical ROM [M6]. The salt must be hashed into both the accept-path and reject-path keys, or binding breaks as it did for round-4 HQC [B25-B28]; salting keeps C2PRI [B23]. Cost: a 2κ-bit public salt in every ciphertext (32 bytes at κ = 128, 64 bytes at κ = 256) and one more hash input. Risk: the result is a different KEM from ML-KEM, so it cannot reuse ML-KEM test vectors or certified implementations as they are. What the salt does not fix: any multi-ciphertext weakness of the lattice problem itself [M12-M14].
2. **FO_M rejection key K̄ = H(z, hpk, c)** gives all MAL binding notions, with a proof, at the cost of hashing 32 more bytes on rejection only [B15]. Same caveat: not byte-compatible with ML-KEM.
3. **Single-seed key storage** (derive d and z from one seed; store only the seed) gives MAL-BIND-K-PK even with standard ML-KEM decapsulation code, if the seed-to-key expansion is Turing's own wrapper around FIPS 203 KeyGen_internal and the decapsulator accepts only seeds, never expanded keys, as secret-key input (Schmieg Prop. 2 [B11]). This keeps ML-KEM compatibility for ek, c and K. RFC 9935's 64-byte (d, z) seed is not enough for MAL-BIND-K-PK [R14].
4. **Protocol-level binding.** Whatever KEM is used, derive the final file key with a KDF over (K, c, ek) (CDM §4.8 [B18]); the hybrid-combiners topic covers how X-Wing does this.
5. **Hedged randomness.** Derive m (and d) as PRF(secret state ‖ OS RNG output ‖ counter), as in RFC 8937 [R10]. This protects against a weak or backdoored RNG without changing the KEM's inputs and outputs; the demonstration in Section 7 shows the receiver needs no change [R17]. Note the difference from what Turing does today: `random::new_key` hashes a 64-byte OS seed with cSHAKE256 so that no raw OS output stays in memory (docs/13 §3). That is a good memory-hygiene measure, but hashing adds no entropy (demonstration [R17], and the FIPS 203 m ← H(m) debate [R8]); hedging needs a secret input the attacker does not know. No paper analyses hedging for ML-KEM specifically (Open questions).
6. **Confirmation codes (verifiable decapsulation, FO_C).** A CRYPTO 2025 transform with IND-CCA proofs in the ROM and QROM that makes a skipped or broken re-encryption check show up as wrong keys in ordinary tests [V6, V7]. It serves the owner's second goal (verified releases) directly. Cost: at most 3.4% for ML-KEM and loss of byte compatibility of K with ML-KEM. NIST lists a 1-byte confirmation code for HQC among the changes under consideration for FIPS 207 [V9].

**Changes that add risk (flag clearly):**

- **Any homemade FO variant.** QROM proofs are fragile and variant-specific; HHK17's own U_m theorems needed a published fix [C7]; Kyber's round-3 H(c) tweak complicated proofs until it was removed [L8, L11]. A variant that is not an instance of a proven transform (HHK17, SFO, FO_M, FO_C) has no security argument. Barbosa-Hülsing show the cost concretely: round-3 Kyber's extra H(c) left levels III and V without a justifying bound [C22].
- **Using the Turing block cipher (or a Turing-based hash) as G, H, J or the XOF.** Every FO proof models these as independent random oracles, and Bellare-Davis-Günther show that getting oracle cloning wrong broke three NIST submissions outright [D1]. SHA-3/SHAKE is the analysed choice. Building a random oracle from a new, lightly analysed 128-bit-block cipher adds an unanalysed assumption to the one place a KEM cannot afford it (lattices-inside-symmetric-primitives topic).
- **Relying on implicit rejection to hide failures.** Inside the KEM, the flag must never leak (error codes, timing, logs): a leaked flag enabled key recovery on FrodoKEM through timing [F2], and side-channel attacks on CCA-secure Kyber exploit the same kind of leak [K6, K10]. But at the file level a wrong key is visible anyway, because the authenticated decryption fails [C24]. So the design must be secure even with explicit rejection. For FO this is supported: Hövelmanns-Kudinov prove explicit rejection as secure as implicit in the ROM up to a rare edge case, and HHM22 give a QROM bound for explicit rejection [C20, C24]. For any new variant this has to be argued again.
- **An IND-CPA lattice scheme with long-term keys** (e.g. to save the re-encryption cost). For Kyber-size keys Qin et al. give lower bounds of 1,216-2,176 queries and practical attacks needing 1,312-2,365 [K8, K9].
- **Parameters or encodings changed "for extra security"** without recomputing δ and the attack cost. A larger message or different compression changes δ, and FO bounds depend on it (decryption-failures and attack-cost topics).
- **Stacking several twists at once.** Each departure from ML-KEM loses the ability to test against NIST ACVP, CCTV and Wycheproof vectors (Section 9) and against other implementations (rust-pqc-implementations topic). Conformance vectors caught all four injected bugs in this session; a custom variant has none until Turing writes its own.

**Concrete recommendation for the design phase (options, not decisions):**
- A. Keep ML-KEM-768/1024 byte-exact inside X-Wing (docs/02), and put Turing's "twist" in the wrapper around it: single-seed key storage, hedged randomness, and a final KDF over (K, c, ek) and a Turing context string. All three are standard-compatible and have published support. ML-KEM's C2PRI, which X-Wing needs, is proven [B24]. Add a stated limit on ciphertexts per long-term key with key rotation (checklist item 21), since the multi-ciphertext security of the lattice problem is unsettled [M12-M14].
- B. If a distinct KEM is wanted, the best-supported option is a salted FO (salt in both key hashes) with FO_M rejection, applied to an unchanged ML-KEM K-PKE (or FrodoPKE), optionally with FO_C confirmation codes. Each ingredient has its own proof: GHS (multi-target IND-CCA), KSW (binding; salted coins do not change their FO results), Starfighters (C2PRI, salted included), Glabush et al. (FO_C). No paper proves the combination (Open question 4), so B needs either a proof written for it or an argument that it is an instance of the proven transforms, and Turing would have to generate its own test vectors.
- In both cases, run the checklist in Section 10 and keep the negative-control tests of Section 9.

## Open questions

1. **Original FO paper: resolved** for the J. Cryptology 2013 full version [C26-C28]. The CRYPTO 1999 version (which the JoC version says had bugs) was not opened.
2. **Explicit-rejection QROM bound: resolved for HHM22** (Corollary 9 transcribed [C20]). Still open: the Kyber team's post writes the old explicit-rejection loss as "q^2 / \sqrt{\delta}", which does not match HHM22's description of prior failure terms (about q_G²·δ_wc); probably a typo in the post (C17 stays UNVERIFIED for the formula). Hövelmanns-Kudinov's eQROM theorem (their §5) was not transcribed either [C24].
3. **HHK17 QROM formula nesting: resolved** by rendering PDF p. 34 [C9]; the earlier reading was wrong and is corrected.
4. **Composition of salted FO and FO_M: partly answered.** KSW show the salt must enter both key derivations (HQC attack, HQC* fix) and that salted coin derivation does not affect their FO^⊥̸ binding results [B25-B27]; Starfighters show salting keeps the C2PRI bounds [B23]. Still no paper was found that states and proves a salted FO_M (rejection key H(σ, hpk, c ‖ salt)) for all MAL notions together with GHS's multi-target IND-CCA theorem. The combination is a reasoned extrapolation, not a proven result.
5. **Machine-checked proof scope: mostly answered.** The paper is classical-ROM, for the draft [C19]; the project repository says its current specification transcribes FIPS 203 (and it does use G(d ‖ k)) and is proved to instantiate an IND-CCA KEM [C25]. Not done here: re-running EasyCrypt, and checking the matrix-index order in the EasyCrypt spec.
6. **Hedging for FO KEMs: still open.** Two further searches (2026-09-27) found no paper that analyses hedged randomness (Bellare 2009 / RFC 8937 style) specifically for ML-KEM or FO KEMs; Boldyreva-Patton-Shrimpton's CCA-setting hedging [R16] is the closest. The reasoning in Section 7 is derived, and the demonstration [R17] shows the mechanism, not a security proof. Settle by a targeted literature search (e.g. "hedged KEM", "KEM randomness misuse") or by writing the argument: if m = PRF(secret, RNG output, ek) with a secret unknown to the attacker, m is pseudorandom even when the RNG output is known, so the KEM sees a uniform m.
7. **Wycheproof "invalid" categories: resolved** [V8]. The 40 unflagged invalid vectors in each mlkem_*_test.json are, by their comments, 10 each of ciphertext too long / too short and private key (the d ‖ z seed, per the schema) too long / too short, which is how the check script treats them.
8. **ePrint numbers: resolved in the continuation pass** [E2, B21]: KMSW explicit-rejection binding 2025/2129; KSW combiners 2025/1416; Fiedler-Günther 2024/702; Connolly et al. "Starfighters" 2025/1397.
9. **CCTV accumulated vectors: resolved.** All three parameter sets match in ipd mode [V5]. Not done: the 1,000,000-test hashes (roughly 7-15 hours each for this pure-Python reference, extrapolated from the 10,000-test times).
10. **Bernstein ePrint 2022/1580: summarised** [M12, M13]. GHS agree with his point about Duman et al.'s claim [M14]. Open: whether his asymptotic multi-ciphertext speedups apply at n ≈ 1000 for concrete parameters (attack-cost-estimation topic is better placed).
11. **Limits of verifiable-decapsulation tests.** A single-author arXiv preprint (Delgado Jiménez, arXiv 2606.04443, June 2026, not peer-reviewed, abstract only read) claims bounds on what confirmation-code tests can certify, including a trade-off "α+β≥1" for execution certifiers over some implementation classes. Not assessed here; read before relying on FO_C as a release gate.
12. **Current HQC transform: mostly answered.** KSW analysed the round-4 HQC salted variant (salt outside the key hash) [B25]. NIST's FIPS 207 slides list the move to a salted transform with (ek_KEM, salt) in the derivation of K [V9], and the pq-families-for-diversity topic reports the HQC specification revision of 2025-08-22. The nist-pqc-standards topic reports no public FIPS 207 draft as of 2026-09-27 (its ledger row 32), so whether the confirmation code will be adopted is not yet known.
13. **Maram-Xagawa, "Post-quantum anonymity of Kyber" (PKC 2023)** is cited by Barbosa-Hülsing and Starfighters as the first direct QROM proof of Kyber's FO variant and an anonymity analysis; it was not opened here.
